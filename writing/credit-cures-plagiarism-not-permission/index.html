Credit Cures Plagiarism, Not Permission

Published August 17, 2026. Revised August 29, 2026.

TL;DR

Citing sources legitimizes borrowing as a creative practice. It does not resolve copyright permissions, and it does not cover transformational creativity that changes domain rules instead of recombining parts.

The August 17, 2026 version is at credit-cures-plagiarism-not-permission-20260817.

In Building a Second Brain (Atria, 2022), Tiago Forte quotes Adam Savage describing kitbashing at Industrial Light & Magic. Model makers raided commercial tank and ship kits, combining loose plastic parts to assemble detailed spaceship hulls quickly.

Forte argues this is a disciplined creative method. Borrow parts rather than whole works: a banner shape, a slide layout, a musical cadence. Citing the source turns borrowing into an attributed contribution rather than stolen work.

The model explains combinatorial work well, but it stops at legal permission and transformational creativity.

The case for combinatorial reuse

The evidence for combinatorial creativity spans distant disciplines. Robert Greene argues that celebrated creative figures were skilled synthesizers rather than pure originators. Synthesis is a learnable skill disguised as innate genius. Kpaxs writes that few ideas start from scratch. Originality usually comes from pairing separate concepts. Reading only within one discipline recycles familiar ideas; reading broadly increases the odds of useful combinations.

Sociologist Niklas Luhmann documented the mechanism in Communicating with Slip Boxes. Over twenty-six years of maintaining a physical slip box, the index produced unexpected connections he never planned: "the query provokes possibilities of making relations which could not be traced prior to it." Julian Shapiro describes the note system as an engine: novelty emerges from unexpected combinations, and high volume feeds the engine enough raw material to generate them.

Physical design relies on the same pattern. King Sejong's 15th-century Hangeul alphabet uses eight basic letterforms placed within a fixed square frame. In a 2024 BusinessWorld piece, curator Kim Eun-jae explains that this square-frame constraint makes Hangeul "highly efficient when used in visual and digital media." I read that as the constraint providing the flexibility.

Even direct copying serves a purpose during study. Jason Cohen notes that Hunter S. Thompson copied The Great Gatsby out by hand to internalize the rhythm of elite prose. His eventual writing style was distinct from Fitzgerald's. The exercise trained technique rather than copying style.

What combinatorial models miss

Citation resolves plagiarism, which is the unacknowledged use of someone else's work. It does not resolve copyright infringement, which is the unauthorized use of protected material.

Duke University's Scholarly Communications office states the difference directly: "Citation... does not cure copyright infringement, which is the unauthorized use of another's work. If you copy an entire journal article by someone else, without permission, into a book you publish, you probably have infringed copyright, even if you add a footnote citing the original author and source." Stanford's Fair Use Center notes the same principle for visual assets: crediting a photographer may support a fair-use defense, but attribution alone does not prevent an infringement claim.

A footnote answers the ethical question of credit. Resolving legal use requires permission, licensing, or statutory fair use.

The second boundary is conceptual. Combinatorial borrowing treats creativity as assembling existing pieces into new configurations. Cognitive scientist Margaret Boden identifies transformational creativity as a separate category, documented across the academic literature on creativity. Transformational creativity does not rearrange pieces within an existing conceptual space. It alters the rules of the space itself, generating ideas that were "previously... literally inconceivable." It still builds on existing foundations, but it does not operate through simple recombination. Combinatorial note systems do not account for rule-changing shifts.

Three questions before the footnote

Before relying on citation to justify borrowed material, evaluate three questions:

  • Is this practice or published work? Thompson's copying of Gatsby was private practice to study form. Hand-copying whole works is legitimate training. It becomes an issue only when published as an original work.
  • Did you take a single element or the whole work? Forte's guideline applies to specific components: a layout, an outline, a structural technique. Borrowing a small component with attribution creates a new contribution. Copying an entire artifact remains someone else's work, regardless of footnotes.
  • Which standard are you addressing? If the concern is attribution and academic honesty, citation satisfies it. If the concern is legal distribution, citation shows good faith, but legal clearance requires licensing, explicit permission, or fair use.

My own notes follow this discipline. Across thousands of markdown files, every synthesized note carries a source footer. Claims without traceable sources are removed. That applies Forte's method mechanically: borrow, cite, and attribute. But citation only addresses the ethical requirement of attribution. Legal rights and genuine paradigm shifts operate under different rules.
