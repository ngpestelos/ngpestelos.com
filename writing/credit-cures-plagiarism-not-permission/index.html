Credit Cures Plagiarism, Not Permission

If you have a production system you need to understand, especially old Rails or wiring agents into a real codebase, write me. Three questions is enough. Or book 25 minutes.

Published August 17, 2026

TL;DR

Citing your sources legitimizes borrowing as a creative practice. It does not legitimize it as a legal one, and it doesn't cover the rarer creativity that rewrites the rules instead of recombining pieces.

I had Claude dig through my notes for everything I'd saved on remix and originality, starting from one on a chapter of Tiago Forte's Building a Second Brain (Atria, 2022), the one where he quotes Adam Savage saying the same handful of kit parts turn up in almost every model Industrial Light & Magic built. Kitbashing: raid a box of commercial tank and ship kits, and a spaceship hull comes out cheap and fast.

Forte's point was that this isn't a shortcut for people who lack originality. Borrow aspects, not the whole, he writes: a banner's shape, a slide's layout, a song's feel. Cite where they came from, and the citing turns borrowing into your contribution instead of someone else's theft.

I wanted to know if that held up. Mostly it does, within the frame Forte draws. What's outside that frame is more interesting than anything inside it.

The case for "mostly"

The case is thick, and it spans fields that rarely cite each other. Robert Greene argues that most celebrated "geniuses" were skilled synthesizers, not originators. "Clever borrower" is a learnable skill; "genius" just makes it sound innate. Kpaxs, on X, goes further: there are no ideas made from scratch, only original pairings. Read only inside your field and you just recycle your field's ideas; breadth is what widens the odds of a surprising one.

The sociologist Niklas Luhmann names the mechanism. In Communicating with Slip Boxes, a translated essay recounting his twenty-six years working with a paper note system, he describes combinations the system produced that he never planned or explicitly stored: "the query provokes possibilities of making relations which could not be traced prior to it." Julian Shapiro frames the same thing as an engine: novelty comes from unexpected combinations, not a flash of inspiration, and volume of attempts is what feeds the engine enough raw material to work with.

Design shows the same pattern outside software and writing. King Sejong's 15th-century Hangeul alphabet gets cited, nearly six centuries later, as proof by design: eight basic letterforms, combined by position within a fixed square frame, generate the full alphabet and its ongoing reuse across calligraphy, fashion, and digital type. Curator Kim Eun-jae, in a 2024 BusinessWorld piece on the alphabet's reinterpretation, calls the square-frame constraint what makes Hangeul "highly efficient when used in visual and digital media." I read that as the constraint being the source of the flexibility, not a limit on it.

And even where the borrowing looks like straight copying, it earns its keep, at least by one account. Jason Cohen, on X, says Hunter S. Thompson copied The Great Gatsby out by hand: not to plagiarize, but to feel what writing at that level took. His eventual style, by that same account, was nothing like Fitzgerald's. The copying built technique, not style.

What "mostly" doesn't cover

Citation cures plagiarism, the unacknowledged use of someone else's work. It does not cure copyright infringement, the unauthorized use of it. Duke University's Scholarly Communications office puts it plainly: "Citation... does not cure copyright infringement, which is the unauthorized use of another's work. If you copy an entire journal article by someone else, without permission, into a book you publish, you probably have infringed copyright, even if you add a footnote citing the original author and source." Stanford's Fair Use Center makes the same point about images: crediting the photographer might count toward a fair-use defense, but it won't protect you from a claim of infringement.

A footnote answers the plagiarism charge. The infringement charge needs permission or a fair-use defense.

This isn't only a professional-publishing concern. The same distinction applies the moment a private synthesis leaves your notes: a blog post, a shared deck, a public repo. "I linked my sources" answers the question a reader asks. It doesn't answer the one a rights holder would.

Everything cited above treats creativity as combination: old pieces meeting in a new order. Margaret Boden, the cognitive scientist behind decades of work on computational creativity, names a category that isn't that. Transformational creativity, an idea corroborated in the wider academic literature on the topic, doesn't recombine the pieces of a conceptual space. It changes the space's own rules, producing thoughts that were, in her words, "previously... literally inconceivable" under the old framework. It's still not creation from nothing. It still starts from something that already exists. But it isn't recombination either. Nothing in Forte's chapter accounts for it.

Three questions before the footnote

So where does that leave the question that matters in practice: knowing when a footnote is enough? Three questions, pulled from the evidence above:

  • Is this the finished, shared piece, or is it practice? Thompson's hand-copied Gatsby, by Cohen's account, was always practice: form study, done to feel what writing at that level took. Copying the whole thing is fine on those terms. It stops being fine the moment it's the output you're crediting yourself for.
  • Are you taking an aspect, or the whole? Forte's own line, from the top of this piece: a banner's shape, a slide's layout, a song's feel. An aspect, credited, is a contribution. The whole, credited, is still someone else's work with your name attached to the footnote.
  • Which question is actually being asked, and is it only one? Publishing anything makes both live at once. If the question is "did you pretend this was original," citation answers it. If the question is "did you have the right to use this," citation is evidence of good faith at best; permission, or genuine transformation, is what actually answers it.

My own notes run on the same rule: a few thousand markdown files, and every synthesized one carries a source footer in the body, because a claim without a traceable source doesn't get to stay. That's Forte's move, just made mechanical: borrow, cite, and let the citation carry the borrowing forward as work instead of theft. What the Duke and Stanford pages showed me is that the rule only ever settled the ethics. The legal question, and per Boden the rarer creative one, was something else entirely.
