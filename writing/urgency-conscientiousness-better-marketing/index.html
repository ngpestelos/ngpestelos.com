Urgency Is Just Conscientiousness With Better Marketing

Published July 28, 2026

In November 2025, an account called Boring_Business posted a claim that has been reposted, quoted, and independently reinvented ever since: "Pretty convinced that the biggest predictor of financial and professional success is sense of urgency. People who take accountability and move fast come out ahead. Even if you have an incredibly high IQ, laziness will block your path to ever reaching the top."

Nobody fact-checked it, because it doesn't feel like a claim that needs checking. It feels like something you already knew.

Three strangers, one claim

At least three people, working from separate observations, landed on close to the same idea within months of each other. One tweet, from someone watching a set of ultra-successful people at close range, lists intense urgency (acting fast, treating time as non-renewable) as one of five recurring traits alongside small trusted circles and unshakeable self-belief, a modest claim, one ingredient among several. Another goes further: elite achievers who treat every objective as time-critical regardless of external deadlines, treating time itself as the competitive edge, closer to the strong, single-cause version Boring_Business posted first.

None of these three are researchers. They are observers describing a pattern they've noticed: no citations, no sample sizes, just conviction, at three different strengths of the same claim.

There's also a sampling problem built into all three: each is describing people they watched succeed. None of them is counting the equally urgent, equally accountable people who tried and didn't make it, so what looks like a real effect could just as easily be survivorship bias.

What the label gets right

Start with the half of the claim that survives contact with actual research: intelligence is not the whole story. That part is true, but not for the reason the tweets imply.

General mental ability is, by a wide margin, personnel psychology's best-validated predictor of job performance. This is not a contested finding. Schmidt and Hunter's 1998 meta-analysis, aggregating 85 years of selection research, put the validity at r=.51 across occupations, well ahead of experience (.18) or education (.10) in their own comparison table. A 2019 re-analysis by Salgado and Moscoso, correcting methodological problems in the original estimate, still found r=.32 to .50 depending on job complexity. Intelligence is not "commoditized" or unreliable. It is one of the most reproducible findings in the field.

So if intelligence is real and validated, why does "smart people fail because they're lazy" keep landing? Because intelligence predicts performance, and nothing in those validity studies measures whether you start. The tweets aren't actually describing an intelligence deficit. They're describing analysis paralysis, perfectionism, and credential-hoarding: a capable person who never converts capability into a finished, shipped thing. That failure mode is real and common. The proposed cure is where the tweets go wrong.

The cure has an existing name

Ask what "urgency plus accountability" would look like as a measured psychological trait, and the closest match isn't a new discovery. It's conscientiousness, one of the Big Five personality traits studied for decades, wearing a name that sounds more actionable than "try being more disciplined."

The clearest test of this comes from grit, Angela Duckworth's popularization of passion-plus-perseverance as a success predictor, because grit and "urgency plus accountability" are describing the same territory. A 2017 meta-analysis by Credé, Tynan, and Harms, spanning 66,807 people, found grit correlates with plain conscientiousness at r=.84, bordering on construct redundancy, and that its predictive power for performance and retention is modest at best. Worse for the "urgency" framing specifically: the "consistency of interest" facet, the part of grit that sounds most like relentless drive, adds close to zero incremental validity once ordinary perseverance is accounted for. The dramatic-sounding half of the trait isn't doing the work.

None of this makes the tweets wrong about the main thing: fast, owned execution beats analysis paralysis. It means the mechanism isn't a mystical "sense of urgency." It's an existing, well-measured trait, and what the tweets are actually recommending is applying more of it to the specific bottleneck of shipping.

Two levers, not one

Neither side wins outright. A 2020 study of leaders and entrepreneurs found cognitive ability weakly predicts who becomes an entrepreneur or reaches top leadership, and correlates with a leader's income, but does not predict entrepreneurial performance on its own. Personality traits accounted for most of the power to predict performance once the role was already won. Ability and disposition are turning out to predict different phases of the same career: who gets the shot, and what they do with it.

Speed has a boundary

The tweets' own prescription also has a boundary condition even the pop version gestures at, just without naming it. Speed helps on decisions you can afford to be wrong about: reversible, high-frequency, low-stakes calls where a wrong answer is cheap information a slow right one never buys you. It does not extend to irreversible ones. Jamie Zawinski's account of building Netscape, recorded in Peter Seibel's Coders at Work (2009), captures both halves in one career: shipping a rough, six-month browser and iterating on real user feedback built market dominance. The identical instinct, applied without discipline to a from-scratch rewrite of the same product, wrecked the company's competitive position against Internet Explorer. The second decision belonged to the class speed was never supposed to touch.

The claim will keep getting reinvented

Capable, observant people keep re-deriving this claim because "high IQ, low output" is a genuine and common failure mode; they're just pointing at something conscientiousness-shaped without knowing its name. The fix isn't a personality transplant into someone dramatically more urgent. It's ordinary follow-through, applied specifically to the decisions cheap enough to get wrong.
