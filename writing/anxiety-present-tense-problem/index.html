Anxiety Is a Present-Tense Problem

Published July 21, 2026

Returning to the present can be engagement or avoidance. If you use 'mindfulness' to hide from the feared thing, it's a safety behavior that feeds the anxiety. The exit is to name the dread, let it stand in the room, and watch it lose the future it was borrowing.

In 2010 two Harvard psychologists, Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert, built an iPhone app that pinged thousands of people at random moments to ask what they were doing, what they were thinking about, and how they felt. A quarter of a million answers later, the finding that landed hardest was this: people's minds are elsewhere about 47% of the time, and that wandering, not the activity itself, predicted how happy they were. Where your attention sits matters more than what is in front of you.

Anticipatory anxiety is one especially expensive place for attention to go.

The worry is now, not later

It feels like information about the future. It is not. When you dread a conversation that hasn't happened or a deadline that hasn't arrived, nothing about the future is reaching you. What is happening is that your mind is running a projection, a threat scenario, and your body is responding to the projection as though it were real. The dread is genuine and it is happening now. The catastrophe it is about is imaginary.

Eckhart Tolle names this directly: anxiety is the mind living in psychological time, an imagined future with no existence outside present thought. Seneca got there nearly two thousand years earlier and said it more plainly, that we suffer more often in imagination than in reality, multiplying our pain past anything the world delivers. Two witnesses, one from a meditation tradition and one from Stoicism, describing the same move: forward projection generating a suffering the future never supplies.

The signal underneath the fiction

This is where the reframe usually overreaches. If the content is imaginary, the tempting conclusion is to be done with worry entirely, to think positive and let it go. But worry is not pure noise. Kate Sweeny's review of the research finds that moderate worry motivates protective action: people who worry the right amount are likelier to prepare, to get the screening, to assemble a plan B. What is fictional is the elaborated catastrophe, the fully rendered scene of everything going wrong. The signal underneath it, that some real thing needs doing, is often accurate. The task is not to silence worry but to keep the signal and drop the movie.

None of this argues against thinking about the future. Sitting down to plan the thing you dread is deliberate, it ends, and it hands you back to the present when it is done. Worry is the version that never resolves: it replays, and no plan closes it. The problem was never looking ahead; it was that looking ahead became a place you could not leave.

Why returning attention works

The cure is inside the diagnosis. You cannot argue with an imagined future, but you can withdraw its audience. The projection runs on present attention; take the attention back, to your breath, your body, the actual task in front of you, and the narrative loses the thing it was feeding on. This means letting the fear be present while you quit supplying the story that inflates it.

There is a measurable mechanism under this. Matthew Lieberman's lab put people in an fMRI scanner and had them label the emotion in distressing images. Naming the feeling, just putting it into words, reduced activity in the amygdala, the brain's threat alarm, and raised activity in a prefrontal region tied to deliberate control. This is why the first move in almost every practice that works is some version of "notice it": name the fear as a present-moment event and the alarm quiets. Jon Kabat-Zinn built an entire clinical program, MBSR, on training exactly this, attention paid on purpose, in the present, without judgment. The skill is not emptying the mind but noticing where attention has gone, and bringing it back again and again.

The catch most advice leaves out

Here is the part the wellness version drops, and it matters most. "Return to the present" can be two different acts that look identical from the outside.

One is engagement: you meet the present moment, the feeling included, and the loop collapses because you have stopped feeding it. The other is avoidance: you use the present as a place to hide from the feared thing, changing the channel every time the dread surfaces. The second one feels like the same advice while doing the reverse. Turning attention away from what you fear, when it functions as escape, is what clinicians call a safety behavior, and safety behaviors are how anxiety sustains itself; each small flight teaches the nervous system that the fear was worth fleeing.

So the practical test is not where your attention went, but whether the fear was allowed to be looked at. If you can put it into words and let it sit there while you breathe, you have made contact, and that is the version that works. If the calm holds only while the fear stays out of view, and the dread springs back the moment the distraction stops, you were hiding. Honest naming is the tell: the plain word you reach only by turning toward the fear, not the tidy label that keeps it at arm's length. You can rarely name a thing that way and still be running from it.

Where this stops

All of this describes ordinary anticipatory worry. It does not describe every anxiety, and the argument would be dishonest if it pretended otherwise.

Two boundaries. First, diagnosable anxiety disorders often invert the advice. Their treatment is exposure, moving toward the feared thing until the nervous system learns the catastrophe never comes; for a phobia or panic disorder, "return to the present" is often the very avoidance that sustains the fear. That work belongs to a clinician, not an essay. Second, some anxiety is not a thought at all. Panic can start in the body: patients whose amygdala is destroyed on both sides still panic on a lungful of carbon dioxide, fear with no story attached. When the alarm rises from the body, the mind cannot reason it back down.

So the claim is narrow. For the everyday dread of a future that has not arrived, the fear is present-tense thought, and the exit is not away from it but through it: name what scares you, let it stand in the room, and watch it lose the future it was borrowing.
