Five Checks Against Motivated Reasoning

Published August 24, 2026. Revised August 29, 2026.

TL;DR

Wanting a thing to be true and it actually being true feel identical from the inside. Five checks catch the gap: name the preference first, ask what would falsify the claim, use a confidence number, apply the same skepticism to sources you like and dislike, and treat certainty itself as a cost. Three of the five trace to published research; two are my own synthesis.

The August 24, 2026 version is at five-checks-against-motivated-reasoning-20260824.

The trap

A fact you carefully checked and a desired outcome feel identical from the inside: both arrive as conviction. Preferences distort reasoning most on the questions we care about most. Five mechanical checks separate evidence from desire without relying on the feeling of certainty.

1. Name the preference before it decides the conclusion

Write down what you want to be true before you check the evidence. Wanting a thing is not evidence for it. The only way to catch a preference smuggling itself into your conclusion is to record the preference first, separately, where you can compare the two afterward. Naming the want stops it from hiding inside the conclusion.

2. Ask what would falsify the claim

Karl Popper proposed falsifiability as the boundary between scientific claims and unfalsifiable belief. A theory counts as testable only if an observation could prove it false. No number of confirmations proves a theory, but a single genuine counterexample refutes it. Applied to everyday beliefs, if no observable outcome could change your mind, the belief functions as an axiom rather than an empirical finding.

3. Assign a number, not a feeling

"I believe it" collapses a 60% probability and a 95% probability into the same phrase. Research from the Good Judgment Project, led by Barbara Mellers and Philip Tetlock, shows that assigning explicit probabilities and tracking them against outcomes over time separates calibrated judgment from confident guessing. Thinking style and update frequency mattered more than raw ability. The most accurate forecasters revised their numbers often in small increments, a pattern explored in "The Addiction to Being Right Is Rarer Than You Think".

4. Check whether the skepticism is symmetric

Ziva Kunda's 1990 research documented how motivation shapes reasoning. Motivation does not shut off critical faculties; it shifts where scrutiny lands. A motivated reasoner scrutinizes disconfirming evidence heavily while accepting confirming evidence with minimal friction. Ask directly: would the same evidence from a source you liked clear your bar?

5. Hold certainty as a cost, not a virtue

Commitment means deciding to act. Certainty means assuming reality cannot prove you wrong. Holding certainty as free makes updating feel like a defeat rather than routine maintenance. This check is a working synthesis, matching the first one.
