Silence as Communication

Reference entry · last updated September 4, 2026

Silence as communication is the stance that language, by its nature, distorts or clouds truth, and that deliberate silence or non-verbal action can convey a truth that words cannot. Three independent traditions converge on this claim by different routes: Taoism, Christian apophatic (negative) theology, and analytic philosophy.[1][2][3] The idea also appears outside philosophy proper — a 2022 television parable states it almost as a thesis statement.[4]

Taoism: the sage who does not speak

Chapter 56 of the Tao Te Ching, attributed to Laozi, opens: "He who knows (the Tao) does not (care to) speak (about it); he who is (ever ready to) speak about it does not know it."[1] The chapter goes on to describe the sage as one who "will keep his mouth shut and close the portals," blunting sharpness and blending with the world rather than explaining it. The claim is not that the sage lacks something to say. It is that readiness to speak about the Tao is itself evidence of not having grasped it — genuine understanding shows up as restraint, not exposition.

Apophatic theology: language failing upward

Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (5th–6th century CE) is usually credited as the source of apophatic, or negative, theology: describing the divine by systematically negating every affirmative statement about it, because no positive description can be adequate.[2] In his Mystical Theology, the ascent toward God is described as a progressive failure of language: "the more it climbs, the more language falters, and when it has passed up and beyond the ascent, it will turn silent completely."[2] Silence here is not an absence but the endpoint of the method — the point where affirmation, negation, and even the negation of negation have all been exhausted.

Analytic philosophy: Wittgenstein's boundary

Ludwig Wittgenstein's Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921) closes with proposition 7, its single most quoted line: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent."[3] The route to this conclusion is logical rather than mystical: the Tractatus argues that meaningful propositions can only picture facts about the world, so ethics, aesthetics, and "the mystical" fall outside what language can state. Wittgenstein's silence and the apophatic tradition's silence are reached by different arguments, but they land in the same place — a boundary past which speaking would misrepresent, so the correct response is to stop.

Applied example: The Old Man (2022)

The Old Man is an FX/Hulu television series that premiered June 16, 2022, based on the novel by Thomas Perry, starring Jeff Bridges as Dan Chase, a former CIA operative living off the grid.[4] In season 1, episode 2 ("II"), Chase recites a parable to Zoe McDonald (Amy Brenneman) while cooking her a meal from his garden:

"This wise old man, he never spoke. It wasn't because he couldn't, or because he had nothing to say. It was because he believed that language deceived. That, by its very nature, it clouded the truth, so it made the world harder to know.

This wise old man, he believed that the truth lived only in silence — communicated by other means. That the food he prepared from his garden conveyed his affection, his gratitude, or his indifference far better than any words could convey.

And it was said that this wise old man could change minds in that way. He could soften the hardest of hearts without ever saying one word."

The monologue restates the same structure found in the Taoist and apophatic traditions — language as a distorting medium, action or presence as the more trustworthy channel — without citing either. It is a fictional character's stated worldview, not a claim the show attributes to a real philosophical source.

What it is not

Silence as communication, in the sense used on this page, is a chosen practice presented as truth-conveying and relationship-building: the wise old man's silence is said to soften hardened hearts. This is a different phenomenon from stonewalling, one of the four communication patterns John Gottman's relationship research identifies as predicting relational breakdown: withdrawing from an interaction, shutting down, and refusing to respond, typically as a reaction to contempt.[5] Both are silence in a literal sense. One is framed as generative and chosen from a position of understanding; the other is reactive, defensive, and associated with relationship deterioration rather than truth.
