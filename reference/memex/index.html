Memex

Reference entry · last updated August 30, 2026

Memex is a hypothetical electromechanical knowledge workstation conceptualized by Vannevar Bush in his 1945 essay As We May Think.[1] Designed to store an individual's complete library of books, records, and communications, the Memex introduced the foundational computing concept of associative indexing, allowing users to build persistent, non-linear trails through disparate documents.[2]

Physical and technical design

Bush conceived the Memex (a portmanteau of "memory" and "index") as a specialized desk containing mechanical, optical, and photographic components:[1]

  • Display screens: Sloped translucent projection plates on the desk surface capable of displaying multiple documents side by side.
  • Microform storage: High-reduction microfilm reels housed inside the desk, capable of storing encyclopedias, textbooks, and correspondence in a compact physical volume.
  • Input mechanism: A scanner plate for digitizing paper documents, photographs, and handwritten marginal notes directly into the microfilm repository.
  • Navigation controls: Keyboard interfaces, code-entry dials, and levers for rapid optical scanning and instantaneous document retrieval.

Associative indexing and trails

The core innovation of the Memex was associative indexing. Bush observed that traditional library filing systems relied on artificial taxonomies (such as alphabetical order or numerical classification numbers) where an item could only reside in one fixed location.[1]

In contrast, the Memex replicated associative cognitive processes:

  • Persistent document linkage: When viewing two related items on adjacent screens, the user entered a code to tie the items together permanently. In the future, opening either item instantly retrieved the linked item.
  • Trail building: A researcher could construct an unbroken path of linked articles, adding personal commentary and cross-references along the journey.
  • Trail sharing: Compiled trails could be duplicated, shared with colleagues, and inserted into other Memex machines to form a distributed network of human knowledge.[3]

Context and motivation

As Director of the Office of Scientific Research and Development during World War II, Bush witnessed the exponential expansion of scientific literature and specialized research. He warned that traditional methods of transmitting and reviewing findings were failing to keep pace with scientific discovery.[1]

Bush proposed the Memex as an augmentation apparatus: a cognitive prosthetic designed to extend human memory and facilitate cross-disciplinary synthesis.

Influence on modern computing

While the electromechanical Memex was never manufactured, its theoretical framework catalyzed several fundamental breakthroughs in computer science:[2]

  • Interactive computing and GUIs: Douglas Engelbart cited Bush's essay as the intellectual foundation for his NLS (oN-Line System) project, which introduced graphical windows, the computer mouse, and collaborative hypertext editing in 1968.[4]
  • Hypertext and Hypermedia: Ted Nelson coined the term hypertext in the 1960s to implement Bush's non-linear trails in digital computation.[5]
  • The World Wide Web: Tim Berners-Lee utilized the associative linking model when designing the Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) and HTML.
  • Personal Knowledge Management: Modern networked note-taking systems explicitly realize Bush's trail-building vision through bidirectional linking, atomic notes, and Maps of Content (MOCs).
