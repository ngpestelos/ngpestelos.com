Food Culture · Cantonese and Hakka Cuisine

Poon Choi

Reference entry · last updated September 24, 2026

Poon choi is a communal feast of southern China in which separately cooked dishes are stacked in layers inside one large basin and eaten from that single container by everyone at the table. The name comes from the vessel: 盆 (poon, or pun) is a basin, and 菜 (choi) is prepared food.[5, 10]

First principles and definitions

The feast is defined by its container. One vessel holds the meal for a whole table, the components are cooked separately before assembly, each by whatever method suits it, and the assembly step places them in a fixed order. The Intangible Cultural Heritage Office of Hong Kong registers the item as Sek Pun (Basin Feast) and describes it as the food culture of the Punti, the local walled villages of the New Territories.[1]

Names in circulation include sek pun, the office's term for the feast; pun choi and puhn choi in Cantonese (Yale pùhn choi, Jyutping pun4 coi3); pun choi in Hakka; and pén cài in Mandarin pinyin. English renderings include big bowl feast, basin cuisine and Canton casserole.[1, 10]

The container is called the poon. Traditional basins were made of wood, at a time when metal vessels were scarce in the villages and wood counted as the highest grade of serving vessel available.[5]

Stacking is a named step. Hong Kong food writers call it 打盆 (da pun), and it carries rules, because the sauces and juices released by the cooked meats drain downward through the basin as the meal proceeds. Ingredients that take up little liquid and keep their shape belong near the top. Ingredients that absorb liquid belong at the bottom, where they end up holding the flavour of everything above them.[5, 6] Absorbency, not cost, sets an ingredient's place in the stack.[5, 10]

The general accounts describe one basin to a table, with each diner taking food from it, and the guidance on serving the dish includes communal chopsticks and spoons for moving food from the basin into individual bowls.[6, 7, 10]

Layer order

The most specific published arrangement is a six-layer list given in a Hong Kong food-science article on the dish's history and evolution. From the top down:[5]

  • Pan-fried prawns and soy-sauce chicken.
  • Fried eel and hand-made dace fish balls.
  • Shiitake mushrooms and dried shrimp.
  • Wai Tau pork, or pork belly braised in fermented red bean curd.
  • Bean curd sticks and squid.
  • Radish and pig's skin.

The same article states the rule behind the list: the top three layers hold dry items such as duck, chicken, dace fish balls and pan-fried prawns, and the bottom three hold ingredients that readily absorb sauce, namely radish, pig's skin and Wai Tau pork, so that the sauce seeps down one layer at a time.[5]

Other accounts describe the same ordering with a different ingredient set. In the general account, radish, pig's skin and bean curd sit at the bottom, pork and dried shiitake mushrooms in the middle, and scarce ingredients such as abalone and sea cucumber at the top, which is where the same physical rule places them, since they hold their shape and take up little of the sauce that migrates downward during the meal.[10]

Vegetables sit outside the arrangement. Walled-village cooks treated vegetables as low-value food, so vegetables and rice or noodles are served separately from the basin, and the general account adds that only a small quantity of vegetable goes into the basin, as a mark of respect to guests.[5, 10]

Preparation as a communal undertaking

Traditional preparation runs across three days, and the work is done by many hands rather than one cook. Village kitchens prepared and cooked the feast in the ancestral hall, and a few masters of standing in the village would take charge of the production, which could run to several hundred basins at one time.[5]

Day one is fuel. Firewood is gathered from the hills and split, a practice that predates bottled gas and survived it, because villagers held that a wood fire burned stronger and held its heat longer than gas and produced a flavour gas could not match. Lychee wood was prized where an orchard stood nearby. Villages switched to gas when the number of tables grew large.[5]

Day two is shopping, and fresh food is the rule. Frozen and chilled products were avoided in part because rural households had no refrigerator, and more strictly because the feast is offered at ancestral halls to the gods and to the ancestors: a spread built from tinned, frozen or ready-to-eat goods counted as disrespectful to them.[5]

The long stew falls on the third day. Pork goes on early in the morning and cooks for a full day, ten hours or more, in the Wai Tau method of pork belly taken through blanching, colouring, seasoning and air-drying, then a hard fire to drive out fat and a slow fire to reduce the liquid. A few decades ago the work also served as a test for a cook, since a wood stove demanded patience and control of the fire.[5]

Recipes travel inside families and villages. Where a method passed from father to son, or was taught among neighbours, it was also withheld from outsiders, which keeps variants tied to lineages and leaves part of the technique unpublished. Wai Tau braised pork is the part of the dish that tests the cook.[5]

The basin itself has changed material. Wood gave way to copper, tin, foil and plastic, and to ceramic, for reasons that include keeping the food hot at the table, the difficulty of storing and repairing wooden vessels, and hygiene, since wood collects dirt. Takeaway versions are packed so that a customer can reheat them at home over a portable gas stove.[5, 6]

Social and ritual function

The official entry lists the occasions: clan ancestor worship, jiao festivals, weddings, lantern lighting ceremonies on the birth of a baby boy, and consecration ceremonies for ancestral halls. It describes the feast as a symbol of unity served to clansmen gathered round a table, and as social recognition of their identities, a centuries-old food culture preserved by Punti clans in the New Territories.[1]

Sidney Cheung, a professor of anthropology at the Chinese University of Hong Kong, groups the same occasions by colour: red events such as weddings, birthdays and homecomings, and white events such as funerals and grave sweeping. The traditional ingredients, among them fish balls, pork, pig's skin, chicken and dried oyster, are ordinary items available all year, so the dish is not built on seasonal food.[4]

Further observances attach to the feast in the village accounts. Villagers held it after the spring and autumn ancestral worship, at the Tin Hau festival alongside dragon and lion dances, at the once-a-year lantern lighting that marked the birth of a son, and at grave sweeping, where the custom of eating on the hillside is called 食山頭.[5, 10] Where the number of tables was large, hosts ran a 流水宴, a flowing banquet that set tables from morning to night so that guests could sit down and eat whenever they arrived.[5]

Two readings of the shared basin appear in the general account. Communal dining that does not distinguish diners by status is read as a value of equality, and a basin feast that does not take place can register that a marriage is not endorsed, which makes the feast a social signal as well as a meal.[10]

Attendance was not managed by invitation. In the traditional form, no cards went out: a red notice posted at the door of the village told relatives and friends that the feast would happen, and the village gathered as one body.[10]

Evidence and limits

Which community the dish belongs to. The Intangible Cultural Heritage Office places the tradition with the Punti (local) walled villages of the New Territories on its entry for the item, in the social practices, rituals and festive events group of the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Hong Kong.[1] The first Representative List was announced on 14 August 2017 with 20 items, among them the sek pun (basin feast), and it grew to 24 items in December 2024.[2, 3] That register is the government's, and it is the authority on how the item is classified. The widely repeated description of poon choi as a Hakka festival meal, which the Wikipedia article carries in its opening sentence, does not match that register.[10] Cheung states that the Hakka adopted the dish only in roughly the past decade, and that it was traditional among Pearl River Delta communities such as Zhongshan, Dongguan and Shunde. Chiu Chow people in the east of the province, he adds, do not typically eat it.[4]

The Song emperor legend. The story that the dish began when villagers served a fleeing Southern Song emperor and his army from wooden washbasins is tradition, and the general account introduces it that way. The Hong Kong food-science article presents it as the most plausible of several competing origin tales and records that the origins are disputed.[5, 10] Neither account presents a document from the period.

Whether it is eaten layer by layer. The general account states that poon choi is typically eaten layer by layer, with the layers sometimes broken to reach the radish at the bottom.[10] Village sources describe the opposite practice. The food-science article records a custom at the basin, 食盆, under which diners do not hold back and together turn the contents over and over, an act read as working together and as fortune turning in one's favour.[5] The Yuen Long village page states the point directly: nobody eats it layer by layer, because the pig's skin, radish and bean curd sticks at the bottom absorb the essence of the meat above them, so the good material settles at the bottom, and diners use communal chopsticks to reach the bottom first.[6] That page also carries a layer-by-layer instruction in its general description of the dish, while its usage tips advise the opposite, so the page is evidence of the dispute as much as of one side of it.[6]

Commercialisation since the 1990s. Restaurants in the New Territories began serving poon choi in the 1990s, aimed first at local tourists, on occasions such as Lunar New Year, the Mid-Autumn Festival and the winter solstice. Urban restaurants followed, and the dish moved upmarket with abalone, fish maw and conpoy. Fast-food chains now sell single portions in a small hollowed-out pumpkin, and the pandemic years pushed the restaurant version into takeaway.[4] Ingredients are portioned exactly per head in the modern version, a dozen servings of each item for a table of twelve, and city restaurants have dropped pork skin, for reasons the same source frames as perhaps health-related, so the commercial dish differs from the village one in portioning, ingredient choice and service.[4]

One Ping Shan business, run by the Tang family for four generations, serves the dish year round rather than only at weddings and Lunar New Year, in a fermented red bean curd gravy and in a large metal basin, and offers a nine-dish banquet as a refined variant presented in separate bowls.[9]

Food safety for a shared vessel

Hong Kong's Centre for Food Safety publishes guidance for the dish, which carries the risks of one container holding many ingredients and passing between many diners.[7]

  • Keep hot poon choi at 60 C or above and chilled poon choi at 4 C or below, which holds it out of the temperature danger zone between 4 C and 60 C.[7, 8]
  • Collect it half an hour to one hour before it is eaten, and go home directly after collecting it.[7]
  • Do not leave it at room temperature for more than two hours after collection or cooking.[7]
  • Reheat it thoroughly, to a core temperature of 75 C or above. A large pot takes longer to heat through than a small one.[7, 8]
  • Consume it in one sitting.[7, 8]
  • Use communal chopsticks and spoons.[7, 8]
  • Home cooks should sterilise cutting boards, knives, containers and other utensils before starting, keep raw and cooked food covered and separate, and cook high-risk items such as poached chicken and fresh oysters thoroughly.[7]
