Vannevar Bush

Reference entry · last updated August 30, 2026

Vannevar Bush (1890–1974) was an American electrical engineer, inventor, and science administrator who headed the U.S. Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD) during World War II.[1] In computing and knowledge management history, he is celebrated for his 1945 essay As We May Think, in which he conceptualized the Memex: a theoretical desk-sized device that pioneered the concepts of associative indexing and non-linear hypertext trails.[2]

Engineering and wartime leadership

Bush served as Professor of Electrical Engineering and later Vice President and Dean of Engineering at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). In the late 1920s and 1930s, he developed the Differential Analyzer, an analog electromechanical computer capable of solving differential equations with up to eighteen independent variables.[1]

In 1941, President Franklin D. Roosevelt appointed Bush Director of the newly created OSRD, coordinating virtually all wartime military research and development in the United States, including radar development, proximity fuzes, mass penicillin production, and the Manhattan Project.[1] In his 1945 report Science, The Endless Frontier, Bush advocated for federal funding of basic scientific research, directly motivating the establishment of the National Science Foundation (NSF).[3]

"As We May Think" and the information problem

Published in The Atlantic Monthly in July 1945, Bush's essay As We May Think addressed the growing crisis of scientific publication and information overload.[2] He observed that human knowledge was expanding exponentially, yet methods for storing, accessing, and finding research remained bound to rigid artificial indexing systems (such as alphabetical filing and numerical library taxonomies).[2]

Bush argued that the human mind does not operate by alphabetical pigeonholes. Instead, memory operates associatively: one thought immediately prompts another along a trail of connections.[2]

The Memex and associative trails

To solve this retrieval crisis, Bush proposed a hypothetical personalized mechanical memory workstation called the Memex (a portmanteau of "memory" and "index").[2]

The Memex was envisioned as a desk equipped with sloped translucent screens, microform storage containing books, periodicals, and personal notes, and input levers for navigation:

  • Associative indexing: The core feature allowed the user to link any two documents together permanently. Selecting an item on one screen would instantly pull up the associated item on the adjacent screen.[2]
  • Trail building: A researcher could build sequential associative paths through disparate literature, adding personal marginalia and passing the compiled trail to colleagues.[2]

Legacy in computing and knowledge management

Although the electromechanical Memex was never constructed physically, Bush's vision directly inspired the pioneers of digital interactive computing and hypertext:

  • Douglas Engelbart: Credited As We May Think as the primary inspiration for his 1968 "Mother of All Demos" (introducing the computer mouse, graphical user interfaces, and collaborative hypertext editing in the NLS system).[4]
  • Ted Nelson: Extended Bush's associative trails into the formal computer science concepts of hypertext, hypermedia, and Project Xanadu.[5]
  • Tim Berners-Lee: Acknowledged Bush's associative model as foundational to the architecture of the World Wide Web.
  • Modern PKM: Bush's associative trails are the direct historical ancestor of bidirectional linking, atomic notes, and Maps of Content (MOCs) in contemporary networked note systems.
