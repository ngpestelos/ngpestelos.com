Romance of the Three Kingdoms

Reference entry · last updated August 27, 2026

Romance of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguo yanyi, 三國演義) is a Chinese historical novel of the Ming period, traditionally attributed to Luo Guanzhong (late Yuan to early Ming). The English word "romance" here translates yanyi, "elaboration" or popular expansion of a chronicle. It is not a love story. The book retells the fall of Eastern Han and the wars of Wei, Shu, and Wu, from the Yellow Turban Rebellion (184) to Jin unification (280), using Chen Shou's third-century Sanguozhi as the backbone and adding folk, drama, and invented scenes.[1]

Title and genre

Early printed titles include Sanguozhi Tongsu Yanyi (Popular Romance of the Records of the Three Kingdoms). Later readers shorten this to Sanguo yanyi. In the conventional list of China's Four Great Classical Novels it sits with Water Margin, Journey to the West, and Dream of the Red Chamber.[1][2]

The work is a long chapter novel (zhanghui xiaoshuo), mixing prose with poems that comment on or close scenes. It is not a substitute for the official histories. Using it as a source for dates, troop counts, or a person's "true character" without checking Chen Shou (and Pei Songzhi's commentary) is a category error.

Origins and attribution

Three Kingdoms stories were already entertainment in the Sui and Tang. Song records mention professional tellers who specialized in those heroes. A printed pinghua (plain tale), Sanguozhi Pinghua, appeared around 1321–1323. Yuan and early Ming plays on the same cycle are mostly fiction on a thin historical thread.[1]

The novel is traditionally assigned to Luo Guanzhong, a playwright placed somewhere between about 1315 and 1400. The first complete printed edition is dated 1522. It carries a preface dated 1494. Manuscripts may have circulated earlier. Luo, or whoever assembled the book, used Chen Shou's Sanguozhi, Tang verse, Yuan drama, and his own arrangement of virtue and legitimacy.[1]

Authorship is traditional, not a signed autograph. This page does not settle debates that add Shi Nai'an or treat Luo as compiler rather than sole author.

The Mao recension

Luo's text in 24 volumes survives in libraries (Shanghai, Tenri, and others). Ming recensions vary in volume count. The text most readers mean is the Qing recension by Mao Lun and his son Mao Zonggang, made in the Kangxi period: 120 chapters, shortened title, heavy cutting and commentary. Chapter titles are couplets. Stories that the earlier 240-hui print kept as paired chronological units are interlaced so parallels and contrasts sit in one chapter.[1]

A late-Ming "Li Zhuowu" commentary (likely Ye Zhou using Li Zhi's art name) had already added interlinear shouts and ironic chapter-end notes. The Mao edition is the one later English complete translations usually follow.

Span of the plot

The narrative opens in the decay of Eastern Han (Yellow Turbans, Ten Attendants, Dong Zhuo) and closes when Jin absorbs the three states. Famous set pieces include the peach-garden oath, the three visits to Zhuge Liang, Red Cliffs, Guan Yu's death, Yiling, and Zhuge's northern campaigns. Liu Bei's circle is the moral center. Cao Cao is the gifted antagonist. That Shu-leaning cast matches Ming political taste more than Jin historiography, which treated Wei as Han's successor.[1]

An example of expansion: Chen Shou's biography treats the southern campaign in a few sentences. The Mao novel stretches it into seven captures and releases of Meng Huo (chapters 87–89 in that recension).[1]

History and fiction

The Qing historian Zhang Xuecheng's line, as repeated in later reference works, is that the novel is "seven-parts fact and three-parts fiction." The ratio is a proverb, not a measured census of chapters. Fictional material comes from unofficial histories, the pinghua, drama, and invention. Social texture and the characters' practical reasoning can still feel of the period even when an episode is false.[1]

Episodes that entered proverb and later games but are not in Chen Shou include the Empty Fort bluff and the straw-boat arrow ruse. The historical Zhuge Liang of the Sanguozhi is a chancellor, logistician, and failed northern campaigner. The novel's infallible wizard is a literary type. Mixing them is the usual error. Detail on that split is on the Zhuge Liang entry.[3][4]

English translations

Charles Henry Brewitt-Taylor published a complete English version in two volumes in 1925. It is readable and has little apparatus. Moss Roberts published an abridgment in 1976 (about a quarter of the text, with maps and notes) and a complete translation in 1991, with Mao commentary in the notes. Yang Ye called Roberts's complete version the text that supersedes Brewitt-Taylor. Yu Sumei and Ronald C. Iverson issued another complete "dynamic" translation (Tuttle, 2014). Martin Palmer's 2018 Penguin abridgment covers all 120 chapters by mixing full scenes with summary.[1]

A Manchu translation of the 1522 text was finished in 1647 and printed in 1650. It is among the earliest dated translations into any language.[1]
