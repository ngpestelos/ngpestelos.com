How to Take Smart Notes

Reference entry · last updated August 30, 2026

How to Take Smart Notes: One Simple Technique to Boost Writing, Learning and Thinking is a 2017 non-fiction book by German education scholar Sönke Ahrens.[1] The book provides an analytical guide to sociologist Niklas Luhmann's Zettelkasten method, arguing that non-fiction writing should proceed as an emergent byproduct of decentralized note aggregation rather than top-down outline construction.[2]

Core thesis and overview

Ahrens posits that the conventional approach to writing non-fiction (starting with a blank page, choosing a thesis, searching for supporting evidence, and outlining) is cognitively backwards.[1] This top-down process creates severe psychological resistance, forces premature conclusions, and amplifies confirmation bias.

Instead, Ahrens advocates embedding note-taking into every reading and research session. By accumulating atomic notes in a networked slip-box, the writer builds a critical mass of interconnected insights. Writing a manuscript then becomes an exercise in assembling existing note clusters rather than inventing text from scratch.[1]

The four categories of notes

The book formalizes a four-tier classification system for information processing:

  • Fleeting notes: Quick, temporary thoughts, voice memos, or marginalia captured throughout the day. These are reviewed within a day or two and either converted into permanent notes or deleted.
  • Literature notes: Brief summaries of external source texts, written in the reader's own words alongside accurate bibliographic metadata.
  • Permanent notes: Self-contained atomic notes that express a single idea with full context. These are filed into the slip-box with explicit associative links to existing notes.[1]
  • Project notes: Temporary outlines, drafts, and administrative to-do lists tied strictly to a specific assignment and archived once the project concludes.

Bottom-up manuscript development

The writing workflow in How to Take Smart Notes follows an iterative sequence:

  1. Capture fleeting thoughts and literature notes while reading.
  2. Synthesize permanent notes and connect them to existing related slips.
  3. Observe emerging topic clusters in the slip-box to identify natural research questions.
  4. Collect related notes into an outline, bridging gaps with focused supplementary notes.
  5. Translate the connected note network into a coherent first draft.

Cognitive principles and bias reduction

Ahrens reinforces his practical workflow with findings from cognitive psychology:

  • Cognitive offloading: Storing structured connections in an external system relieves working memory, preventing mental exhaustion during complex synthesis.[3]
  • Confirmation bias reduction: Because notes are filed before a final thesis is chosen, researchers collect contradictory facts and counterarguments with equal enthusiasm.
  • The Zeigarnik effect: Writing down an insight closes open mental loops, preventing background anxiety while allowing researchers to pause tasks without losing momentum.[1]

Influence on knowledge management

Upon publication in 2017 and subsequent revision in 2022, How to Take Smart Notes became the primary intellectual catalyst for the networked thought movement.[4] It inspired the development of modern hypertext tools including Roam Research, Obsidian, and Logseq, and laid the theoretical groundwork for modern evergreen notes and Maps of Content (MOCs).[5]
