Linking Your Thinking

Reference entry · last updated August 30, 2026

Linking Your Thinking (LYT) is a personal knowledge management framework developed by Nick Milo that organizes notes through fluid associative linking and curated hub notes rather than rigid folder taxonomies.[1] The system prioritizes bottom-up concept emergence and sensemaking over static archival storage.

Core philosophy and design principles

Traditional digital filing systems force information into mutually exclusive hierarchical folders. In practice, this produces categorization anxiety (hesitating over where a note belongs) and folder sprawl (creating excessive nested directories that hide content).[2]

Linking Your Thinking replaces rigid hierarchies with three core tenets:

  • Fluid structure: Notes reside in shallow folders, while semantic relationships are expressed through bidirectional links.
  • Non-destructive organization: An idea can connect to multiple concepts without copying files or creating fragile symlinks.
  • Thinking over filing: The organizational process itself is treated as a cognitive act that develops understanding.

Maps of Content as the primary navigational layer

The central mechanism of the LYT framework is the Map of Content (MOC). An MOC is a dedicated note that acts as a curated index and workbench for a specific cluster of atomic notes.[1]

Within LYT, MOCs operate across multiple levels of abstraction:

  • Home note: The top-level launchpad linking to major life domains and core interests.
  • Domain MOCs: High-level overviews mapping broad subject areas (such as Psychology, Engineering, or Philosophy).
  • Topic and Project MOCs: Tightly scoped maps curating focused inquiries, arguments, or creative deliverables.

The ARC workflow

LYT structures knowledge lifecycle management around three sequential phases, termed the ARC framework:[3]

  • Add: Ingesting raw source materials, highlights, and fleeting observations into an inbox space without immediate categorization pressure.
  • Relate: Synthesizing raw inputs into concise atomic notes, linking them to existing notes, and organizing them into relevant Maps of Content.
  • Communicate: Leveraging assembled MOC clusters to draft articles, design products, or make informed decisions.

Comparison with other methodologies

LYT occupies a distinct position among personal knowledge management methodologies:

  • Versus PARA (Tiago Forte): PARA organizes files strictly by actionability (Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives). LYT organizes notes by associative meaning and conceptual affinity, using MOCs to bridge across project boundaries.[4]
  • Versus Classic Zettelkasten (Niklas Luhmann): While both rely on atomic notes and bidirectional links, Luhmann used alphanumeric branching card numbers in a single physical slip-box. LYT adds higher-order MOC hub notes to make digital graph navigation structured and deliberate.[5]
