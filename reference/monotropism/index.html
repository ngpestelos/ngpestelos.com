Monotropism

Reference entry · last updated September 21, 2026

Monotropism is a theory of autism that explains autistic traits as consequences of how attention is allocated. A monotropic mind concentrates its scarce attention on a small number of interests at any moment, leaving fewer resources for everything outside them.[1][2]

1. First principles: attention as a scarce resource

The theory starts from a constraint: at any one moment the amount of attention available to a conscious individual is limited. Dinah Murray, Mike Lesser, and Wendy Lawson ground this in the finite supply of metabolites available to the brain and in the experimental-psychology concept of task demand. They treat competition between mental processes for scarce attention as a factor that shapes cognition itself.[1]

Allocation can then be parameterized. Attention may be broadly distributed over many interests, or concentrated in a few, with the extremes analogous to diffused light and a torch beam. The 2005 paper proposes that allocation strategies are normally distributed and largely genetically determined, and that a diagnosis of autism selects individuals at the tight-focus extreme of that distribution.[1]

The two ends of the axis are named. A monotropic tendency means having few interests highly aroused; a polytropic tendency means having many interests less highly aroused. An aroused interest is one charged with feeling, and the authors use the word interest in its ordinary sense.[1] Later work describes the same construct as a continuum from monotropic to polytropic predispositions rather than a binary type.[5]

2. The theory: attention tunnels and interests as basins of attraction

When attention commits to one channel, the state is described as an attention tunnel. Processing inside the tunnel is deep and absorbing, and information outside it is heavily filtered.[2]

Interests are modeled as attractors. A monotropic interest is much more closed than a typical interest: monotropic interests are deep basins of attraction where attention gets caught, may be expressed in a thought or action over and over again, and may leave no alternative attractor apparent.[1] The paper states the consequence bluntly: monotropism results in large areas of potential information not being registered.[1]

The model does not predict a general preference for local over global processing. It predicts hyper-awareness within the attention tunnel and hypo-awareness outside it, which the authors offer as an account of the hyper- and hypo-sensitive sensory responses reported in autism.[1]

3. How the theory accounts for autistic traits

The 2005 paper argues that the restricted range of interests named in the third diagnostic criterion is central to autism, and that the first two criteria, covering social interaction and communication, follow from it.[1]

On social interaction, the stated reason is the patchy and partial awareness that results from monotropic focus. Social discourse requires participants to track a shifting common interest turn by turn; a monotropic person may not register another person's interests unless those interests enter the tunnel.[1]

On routines and change, the theory treats resistance to change as a property of deep self-determined attention tunnels. Every unanticipated change seems abrupt and requires time for adjustment, and to a person inside the tunnel an unanticipated change is a complete disconnection from a previous safe state.[1]

On communication, unusual features are traced back to monotropic perception and thought patterns that fragment understanding, so that elements which seem obvious under diffuse attention are missed.[1]

On overload, later descriptions of the theory state that it becomes difficult to interact with stimuli outside the attention tunnel, and that breaking the tunnel can cause meltdowns.[5]

The model also predicts an uneven skills profile. Because learning a skill requires having an interest in doing so, and because monotropic attention yields a fragmentary view of the world, the authors expect strong performance inside fired interests and gaps elsewhere.[1]

4. Measurement: the Monotropism Questionnaire

For two decades monotropism was discussed largely as a conceptual model. The Monotropism Questionnaire (MQ) was built to measure it. The instrument has 47 items generated by a group of autistic adults from lived experience and academic expertise. A validation study collected responses from 1,110 participants, 756 autistic and 354 non-autistic, alongside two established short autism measures, the RAADS-14 and the AQ-10. Exploratory factor analysis suggested an eight-dimensional structure with high internal consistency, and the measure showed good convergent validity with both comparison scales (all correlations above .61). Autism status and self-reported ADHD status were each associated with higher mean monotropism scores.[3]

The authors describe this as the first systematic attempt to develop and validate a measure of monotropism, and call for further testing across demographic and diagnostic groups before the results are generalized.[3]

5. Status and limits

The term was introduced by Dinah Murray in 1992 and formalized in the 2005 paper, which is a theoretical synthesis rather than an empirical study. The authors state that they are not attempting to establish new facts about autism but trying to interpret what is already known, integrating prior research under a single model of attention allocation.[1] The theory was developed by autistic people, and the project literature describes it as the most prominent theory of autism developed and led by autistic people.[5]

Monotropism is presented as a unifying account. The 2005 paper compares it with three earlier cognitive explanations of autism, theory of mind, weak central coherence, and executive dysfunction, and argues that the findings those accounts rest on are equally well explained by monotropic attention allocation. It notes that weak central coherence is the closest of the three to its own position.[1]

Several limits are stated by the theory's own authors and maintainers. The validation study for the MQ is published as an OSF preprint rather than in a peer-reviewed journal.[3] The questionnaire is not an autism assessment: it measures degree of monotropism, and it remains too early to say whether all autistic people are monotropic or whether all monotropic people are autistic. How ADHD fits the picture is also unclear, although the validation sample showed higher monotropism scores among participants with ADHD.[3][4] The theory's maintainers note that a reader does not need to accept monotropism as a general theory of autism for it to be a useful description of common autistic experience.[2]
