Biography · Knowledge Management

Nick Milo

Reference entry · last updated August 30, 2026

Nick Milo is an educator, writer, and practitioner in personal knowledge management (PKM). He is the creator of the Linking Your Thinking (LYT) framework and the originator of the Map of Content (MOC) methodology in modern networked note-taking software.[1]

Background and career

Prior to his work in knowledge systems, Milo worked in television production, video editing, and digital storytelling. His background in non-linear video editing informed his approach to information architecture, emphasizing spatial arrangement, modular scene assembly, and non-destructive organizational workflows over rigid folder filing.[2]

With the release of local-first, markdown-based networked note-taking applications like Obsidian in 2020, Milo emerged as a leading voice advocating for bottom-up, graph-based knowledge organization.[1]

Linking Your Thinking framework

Milo developed the Linking Your Thinking framework to address what he termed "digital friction" and "categorization fatigue" in traditional folder-based note-taking systems. While classic filing structures force users to choose a single location for every note, LYT utilizes bidirectional hypertext links and fluid hub notes to enable organic emergence.[3]

The system emphasizes that knowledge work should center on thinking and connection rather than administrative filing maintenance.

Map of Content methodology

Milo's most influential conceptual contribution is the Map of Content (MOC). An MOC is an associative index note that gathers and curates links to discrete atomic notes around a specific topic, inquiry, or project.[3]

Unlike rigid folder structures, MOCs provide several operational benefits:

  • Non-destructive linking: A single atomic note can appear in multiple MOCs simultaneously without duplicating files on disk.
  • Flexible entry points: Higher-order MOCs organize parent domains while specialized sub-MOCs curate focused lines of inquiry.
  • Thinking workbenches: MOCs serve as active workspaces for synthesizing notes into outlines, articles, and long-form publications.

The Ideaverse and ARC model

In subsequent iterations of his curriculum, Milo formalized the broader workspace architecture as the Ideaverse, structured around the ARC lifecycle:[1]

  • Add: Gathering raw inputs, fleeting thoughts, and external sources into an intake area.
  • Relate: Connecting notes, extracting atomic insights, and linking them into structured Maps of Content.
  • Communicate: Assembling curated note clusters into outward creative outputs, essays, or products.
