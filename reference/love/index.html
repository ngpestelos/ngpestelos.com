Love

Reference entry · last updated October 7, 2026

Love is the complex stance of valuing another person or entity, manifested through emotional concern, dedicated action, and care for their flourishing.[1] Across philosophical traditions and empirical psychology, love is treated not as a passive feeling or transient mood, but as an active volitional orientation that alters an agent's reasons for action, prioritizes the beloved's good for their own sake, and binds identity across time.[2]

1. First Principles and Axioms

A rigorous account of love identifies three structural axioms:

  1. Non-instrumental valuing: The lover cares about the beloved for the beloved's own sake, rather than merely as an instrument for utility, prestige, or personal pleasure.[1]
  2. Particularity and non-fungibility: Love attaches to a particular individual rather than an interchangeable bundle of abstract characteristics. The beloved cannot simply be traded for another agent who possesses superior traits.[2]
  3. Vulnerability to fortune: By tying personal wellbeing to the condition of another, love expands an agent's exposure to grief, injury, and sorrow.[2]

2. Classical Taxonomy of Love

Classical Greek philosophy differentiated the multiple dimensions of love into precise categories:[1]

  • Philia: Mutual affection, deep companionship, and reciprocal goodwill between friends, peers, or fellow citizens who share common pursuits and values.
  • Eros: Passionate desire, longing, and romantic attraction, characterized in Platonic thought as a pursuit of transcendent beauty and union.
  • Agape: Unconditional, self-giving love that extends benevolent goodwill without demanding reciprocity or consideration of merit.
  • Storge: Natural familial affection and instinctive loyalty, such as the bond between parents and offspring.

3. Volitional and Valuing Theories

Philosopher Harry Frankfurt articulated the volitional account of love in The Reasons of Love.[2] Frankfurt contends that love is a disinterested concern for the flourishing of the beloved that is not under our direct cognitive control, yet constitutes the ultimate foundation of practical reason.

For Frankfurt, love creates practical necessities: it determines what matters to an agent, providing reasons for choosing one course of action over another that bypass abstract moral calculations.[2]

4. Psychological Models

Empirical psychology examines love through structured components and attachment dynamics.

Robert Sternberg proposed the triangular theory of love, positing that love can be understood across three intersecting axes:[3]

  • Intimacy: Feelings of closeness, connected bonding, and mutual disclosure.
  • Passion: Drives that lead to physiological arousal, romantic desire, and physical attraction.
  • Commitment: The cognitive decision to maintain loving engagement through time and difficulty.

Varying combinations of these components yield different relationship expressions, with consummate love representing the balanced integration of all three.[3]
