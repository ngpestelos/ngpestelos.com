How Do You Know It's True?

The fancy word for this is epistemology. The question underneath it is one you already ask, just not out loud: how do I know I'm not just believing what I want to believe?

The big word, unpacked

Epistemology just means "the study of how you know things." That's it. No test at the end.

The trap: wanting-true and being-true feel identical

A fact you carefully checked and a fact you're just hoping for both arrive in your head wearing the same badge: "I'm sure." You can't tell them apart from the inside, in the moment.

Why this matters

Your preferences are strongest exactly on the questions you care most about — which is exactly where they distort your reasoning the most. The fix below isn't "trust yourself less in general." It's four specific checks that don't rely on the feeling of certainty at all.

Check 1 — say what you want, before you look

Write your preference down first. Then you can catch it later if it snuck into the answer.

Check 2 — ask what would prove you wrong

If nothing could ever change your mind, the claim isn't knowledge. It's a belief wearing knowledge's clothes.

Check 3 — use a number, not a feeling

"I believe it" hides whether you mean 60% sure or 95% sure. Naming the percent forces the difference back into view.

Check 4 — use the same ruler for every source

If you'd wave off the same kind of evidence from a source you dislike, the problem isn't the source. It's the ruler.

Why this one catches people off guard

It's rarely obvious in the moment. Most people who do this believe they're being appropriately careful with the source they distrust — the tell is only visible when you compare the two standards side by side, after the fact.
