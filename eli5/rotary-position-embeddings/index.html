How Does RoPE Mark Position?

RoPE turns query and key arrows by their token positions so their match includes how far apart they are.

What these arrows stand for
  • Attention compares a query with a key. Each is a list of numbers made from token content.
  • Two numbers form one arrow. RoPE rotates these pairs inside queries and keys. The original Transformer adds position encodings to token embeddings.
  • This picture holds the unrotated arrows fixed to isolate position. Actual token content also changes the match.

The gap changes the match

For these fixed arrows, a wider gap changes the angle between them and their pair score.

Why a score can be negative
  • For unit arrows, the dot product measures alignment: 1 for the same direction, 0 at a right angle, and −1 for opposite directions.
  • This pair score contributes to an attention score. Attention combines many pairs, scales the result, and converts scores into weights; the displayed number is not an attention probability.

Move both, keep the gap

Adding three to both positions turns both arrows equally, keeping their relative angle and pair score.

When equal gaps give equal scores
  • The identity is R(m)ᵀR(n) = R(n − m): the two rotations reduce to their difference.
  • Equal gaps preserve the dot product when the unrotated query and key content stays fixed. Different content can give different scores at the same gap.

Several pairs turn at different speeds

RoPE uses fast and slow rotations across many pairs to represent position at several scales.

Repeats and real context limits
  • One rotating pair repeats after a full turn. At the toy speed of 30° per token, positions 12 tokens apart line up again.
  • The original RoPE schedule uses θᵢ = 10000^(−2i/d) radians per token for pair i, where i starts at zero and d is the query/key dimension.
  • Multiple frequencies give different positional scales. Reliable use of long context also depends on training and the model; rotation alone does not guarantee it.
