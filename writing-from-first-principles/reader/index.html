0. The Reader

Everything in this guide follows from eight facts about the reader and one rule they force. Read this page first. Each chapter names the two facts it rests on.

Eight facts

Facts 1 and 2 govern the essay. Facts 3 and 4 govern the paragraph. Facts 5 and 6 govern the sentence. Facts 7 and 8 govern the reader building a model of someone else's text.

  1. The reader can stop at any sentence. The point cannot wait for the end. Each paragraph must buy the next.
  2. The reader does not have your head. The order of the text is the order they need things in, not the order you found them.
  3. The reader holds one idea in focus at a time. A paragraph carries one idea, or the reader holds neither.
  4. The paragraph break is where the reader decides. Every break is a promise: one idea has ended, another begins.
  5. Working memory holds about twenty words. A sentence must resolve before its opening fades.
  6. The reader parses by finding who did what. The actor and the verb come early, and nothing hides them.
  7. Comprehension is a model, not decoding. Every word can be read while nothing is understood.
  8. The model is built in working memory. Details need slots before they arrive, or they fall out.

One rule

Give the reader what they hold, then what they need, and stop.

At essay scale: say the claim first, then answer the reader's objections in the order they arise. At paragraph scale: claim in the first sentence, evidence and mechanism in the middle, hand-off at the end. At sentence scale: known first, new last, verb early. In reading: build the slots, fill them, prove the model by producing it.

The case file

One case runs through every chapter, taken from the author's own record.

  • 20260901. A founder interview for an AI engineer seat at a small AI startup. The rejection text said the answers were "very high-level" and "missed finer details." Forty-three prep files had produced abstraction.
  • 20260904. A journal entry moved, in one line, from "leads have not panned out" to "no one wants to hire me," then forecast a world with fewer programmers.
  • 20260905. The day's review named the pattern (a specific puncture widened to a life verdict) and read the entry's prose: tight in observation mode, loose in forecast mode. An author draft opened the same day, claim "Employers will hire fewer programmers," cap 800 words.

The chapters take sentences from that entry apart and rebuild them, use the rejection as the running claim, and use the draft as the place where the three writing chapters meet.

The no-model protocol

This guide exists to make its reader less dependent on language models. Every test in it is a pen test: summarize in one sentence, underline first noun phrases, read the last three words, reproduce without the page. A model can do none of these for you without removing the thing being trained.

  1. No model touches a text until you have run the chapter's tests yourself and written the result down.
  2. After that, a model may act as a second reader only, in detect mode. It reports what it finds. It does not rewrite.
  3. It works only on the finding you already wrote. "Is the mechanism in paragraph three missing?" is allowed. "Fix paragraph three" is not.
  4. For reading, the reproduction (chapter 1, step 6) is written before any summary is requested. A model's summary is then put next to yours, and the text decides which one it supports.

How to use the sequence

Reading comes before writing because recognition comes before production. You learn to see a claim, an objection, and a mechanism in someone else's text before you can put them in your own.

The writing chapters run top-down, essay to sentence, on purpose. A model is strongest at the sentence and useless at the claim. Fluent sentences with no claim is the exact dependence this guide is meant to end. Claim first, then objections, then polish. Each chapter ends with an exercise on your own text from the last week, never on a text you asked a model to write.
