0. The Reader

Everything in this guide follows from eight facts about the reader and one rule they force. Read this page first. Each chapter names the two facts it rests on.

Eight facts

Facts 1 and 2 govern the essay. Facts 3 and 4 govern the paragraph. Facts 5 and 6 govern the sentence. Facts 7 and 8 govern the reader building a model of someone else's text.

  1. The reader can stop at any sentence. The point cannot wait for the end. Each paragraph must buy the next.
  2. The reader does not have your head. The order of the text is the order they need things in, not the order you found them.
  3. The reader holds one idea in focus at a time. A paragraph carries one idea, or the reader holds neither.
  4. The paragraph break is where the reader decides. Every break is a promise: one idea has ended, another begins.
  5. Working memory holds about twenty words. A sentence must resolve before its opening fades.
  6. The reader parses by finding who did what. The actor and the verb come early, and nothing hides them.
  7. Comprehension is a model, not decoding. Every word can be read while nothing is understood.
  8. The model is built in working memory. Details need slots before they arrive, or they fall out.

One rule

Give the reader what they hold, then what they need, and stop.

The rule has three parts. What they hold is what the reader already has in mind: the last thing you said, or the thing they came with. What they need is the one new thing this sentence, paragraph, or essay must add. Stop means you end on that new thing, with nothing trailing after it.

Take it apart on one line from Rosa's rent letter. She wrote:

"It is worth noting that, over the course of the last few months, the shop has unfortunately experienced a significant drop in weekday customers, which as you can imagine has been difficult, at least in the mornings."

What the landlord holds: the shop, and the fact that this letter is about rent. He does not hold "worth noting," "over the course of," or "as you can imagine." Those words spend his attention on nothing, and they come first.

What he needs: one fact about customers, and its size. The fact is there, buried in the middle and wrapped in padding: "a significant drop in weekday customers." Significant is not a size.

Where it should stop: at the fact. Instead the sentence runs on into "at least in the mornings," so the last thing he reads is a qualifier, and a qualifier is what he carries to the next sentence.

Rebuilt under the rule:

"The shop lost 140 weekday-morning sales in the month after the parking rules changed."

The shop: what he holds, first. Lost 140 weekday-morning sales: what he needs, with a number in place of an adjective. In the month after the parking rules changed: the cause, last, because the next sentence will pick it up. Nothing trails. Fourteen words instead of thirty-seven, and the landlord can check every one of them.

The same rule at each scale:

  • Essay. What they hold is the problem they came with. What they need is the claim, then the answers to their objections in the order they raise them. Stop is a close that gives one consequence and no summary.
  • Paragraph. What they hold is the first sentence, a claim that links to the paragraph before. What they need is the evidence and the mechanism. Stop is the hand-off.
  • Sentence. Known first, new last, verb early, nothing trailing.
  • Reading. The rule run backward. Build the slots (what you hold), fill them from the text (what it gives), and stop when you can reproduce the model without the page.

The case file

One case runs through every chapter. It is invented, and ordinary on purpose: a baker, a landlord, and a letter that did not work.

  • Day 1. Rosa, who runs a small bakery, writes to her landlord to ask for a lower rent. The reply says the letter was "very general" and "missed the specific numbers." Three weeks of drafting had produced a plea, not a case.
  • Day 4. Her diary moves, in one line, from "two regulars stopped coming this month" to "the whole street is dying," then forecasts a town with no small shops.
  • Day 5. Rereading, she names the pattern (two lost customers widened to a verdict on the town) and sees the prose: tight where she describes the shop, loose where she predicts. She opens a new draft the same day, claim "The street is losing shops to parking, not to prices," cap 800 words.

The chapters take sentences from that diary apart and rebuild them, use the landlord's reply as the running claim, and use the draft as the place where the three writing chapters meet.

The no-model protocol

This guide exists to make its reader less dependent on language models. Every test in it is a pen test: summarize in one sentence, underline first noun phrases, read the last three words, reproduce without the page. A model can do none of these for you without removing the thing being trained.

  1. No model touches a text until you have run the chapter's tests yourself and written the result down.
  2. After that, a model may act as a second reader only, in detect mode. It reports what it finds. It does not rewrite.
  3. It works only on the finding you already wrote. "Is the mechanism in paragraph three missing?" is allowed. "Fix paragraph three" is not.
  4. For reading, the reproduction (chapter 1, step 6) is written before any summary is requested. A model's summary is then put next to yours, and the text decides which one it supports.

How to use the sequence

1  Reading             recognize claim, objection, evidence, mechanism in others' text
2  The Essay           claim and cost, the objection chain, opening and close
3  The Paragraph       first sentence, two questions, hand-off
4  The Sentence        spine, known-first new-last, seven cuts
5  The Practice Card   the twelve tests on one page; test top-down, fix bottom-up
6  Glossary            the terms, one definition each

Reading comes before writing because recognition comes before production. You learn to see a claim, an objection, and a mechanism in someone else's text before you can put them in your own.

The writing chapters run top-down, essay to sentence, on purpose. A model is strongest at the sentence and useless at the claim. Fluent sentences with no claim is the exact dependence this guide is meant to end. Claim first, then objections, then polish. Each chapter ends with an exercise on your own text, written before you read the chapter and never by a model.
