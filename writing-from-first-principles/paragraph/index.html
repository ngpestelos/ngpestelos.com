3. Paragraph Construction

Part 1. Two facts about the reader, and what a paragraph is

Uses Facts 3 and 4 from The Reader: one idea in focus, and the break is where the reader decides.

Fact 3. The reader can hold one idea in focus at a time. Not two. A paragraph that carries two ideas leaves the reader holding neither, and they will not tell you; they will reread, or skim, or leave.

Fact 4. The paragraph break is where the reader breathes and decides. White space is a signal: "one idea has ended, another begins, you may stop here or continue." Every break is a promise about what came before and what comes next. A break in the middle of an idea breaks the promise. No break across two ideas breaks it the other way.

From these, what a paragraph is:

One idea, stated in its first sentence, supported in the middle, handed off at the end.

A paragraph argues where a sentence only asserts. It is the smallest unit of writing that can be checked: does the support carry the first sentence? That is why the essay chapter made each paragraph one step of the objection chain. The paragraph is where a claim meets its evidence.

Length follows from the idea, not from a rule. A paragraph is as long as the support the first sentence needs, then it breaks. On a screen that is usually three to five sentences. The resolution hierarchy's drill of ten or more sentences is a training constraint to build stamina for holding one idea; it is not a target for finished prose. For a reader who scans (my own reading adaptation), shorter paragraphs with a clear first sentence are the accessible form.

The test you will use most: summarize the paragraph in one sentence. If the summary needs "and," there are two paragraphs. If you cannot summarize it at all, there is no idea yet, only sentences near each other.

One consequence to use today. Rosa's day-4 diary paragraph that ran from "I see the whole street changing" through "no small shop will last" carried three ideas: a forecast about the street, a forecast about customers, and a claim about her own shop. Three first sentences were hiding in one block. Split, each would have had to stand on its own support, and the weak one would have shown.

Part 2. The first sentence: the paragraph's claim

Fact 3 says the reader holds one idea. Fact 4 says they decide at the break whether to continue. Put those together and the first sentence has three jobs, in this order of weight:

  1. State the idea. The whole paragraph in one sentence.
  2. Tell the reader whether to read on. A reader who scans reads first sentences and nothing else. If the first sentences of a page do not read as an outline of the argument, the page has no outline.
  3. Connect to the paragraph before. One element the reader already holds, then the new thing.

The first sentence is a claim, not a topic and not a transition. "There are several reasons the letter failed" is a topic; it promises a list and states nothing. "The letter failed because it had no numbers" is a claim; it can be denied and it announces its own support.

Not a first sentence: a question (unless sentence two answers it), a quotation, a bare fact with no claim on it ("In March the parking rules changed"), or a connective standing alone. "However" may open the sentence, but only as the head of a claim.

Where the idea sits inside the sentence matters. Readers give most weight to the end of a sentence. Compare: "Because it had no numbers, the letter failed" and "The letter failed because it had no numbers." The first is a paragraph about failure. The second is a paragraph about the missing numbers. Put at the end the thing the paragraph will develop.

The hinge. Cover the previous paragraph with your hand and read the first sentence. It should still make sense on its own, and it should also show it is a continuation: one word or phrase the reader already knows from the paragraph above, then the new claim. That known-then-new order is the same rule that will run inside the paragraph (Part 3) and inside the sentence (the next chapter).

One exception, the same as in the essay chapter: a narrative paragraph may open with the scene. The idea is implied and the writer knows it. Everywhere else, the first sentence is the claim.

Live example. Part 1 found three ideas in one day-4 block. As three first sentences:

  1. "Drivers buy most of the bread on this street, and parking decides where they stop."
  2. "The bakery is losing its weekday customers, because the new parking rules moved their cars two blocks away."
  3. "A second kind of customer is appearing, the one who walks from the new flats, and the bakery has not learned to serve them yet."

Each can be denied. Each says what its paragraph must prove. Read in a row, they are an outline. The third one also shows the hinge: "a second kind of customer" is new, and it leans on "customers" from the sentence before.

Test: read only the first sentences of a page. If they do not read as an outline, find the paragraph whose first sentence is a topic, a fact, or a question, and rewrite it as a claim.

Part 3. The middle: evidence, mechanism, and the known-to-new chain

The first sentence made a claim. The reader now asks two questions, in order: "How do you know?" and "Why does that follow?" The middle of the paragraph is the answer to those two questions and nothing else.

Two kinds of sentence, then:

  • Evidence answers "how do you know." One specific thing: a number, an incident, a quote, a file, a date. An adjective is not evidence. "The results were strong" is a claim wearing evidence's clothes.
  • Mechanism answers "why does that follow." It is the causal link between the evidence and the claim. Forms: "because," "which means," "so," "that is why." This is the sentence writers skip. Evidence without mechanism is a number and a request to trust you.

How much evidence. One piece per claim, usually. Two when the first is contestable. Never a list of five: the reader does not add weak evidence up, they notice the weakest item and discount the rest. The strongest single piece beats three ordinary ones.

The chain. Inside the middle, every sentence begins with something the reader already holds and ends with something new. The known part comes from the sentence before it, or from the first sentence. If a sentence opens with something new, the reader must hold two things at once, and Fact 3 says they cannot. This is the rule from Part 2 running at sentence scale.

Two chain shapes, and a paragraph should pick one:

  • Linear. Each sentence picks up the end of the one before: A leads to B, B leads to C, C leads to D. Use it for causal steps and for mechanism.
  • Hub. The first sentence's subject stays the subject of every supporting sentence: A then B, A then C, A then D. Use it for parallel evidence about one thing.

A paragraph that switches shapes at random is what readers call disorganized. They cannot say why; the shapes switched.

Not in the middle: a second claim (that is a new paragraph), a hedge ("of course, this may not always hold"), a restatement of the first sentence in other words, and background the reader did not ask for. Each of those is a sentence that neither answers "how do you know" nor "why does that follow."

Live example. First sentence 2 from Part 2: "The bakery is losing its weekday customers, because the new parking rules moved their cars two blocks away."

Evidence: "The month after the rules changed, the till showed 140 fewer weekday-morning sales, and the Saturday count did not move."

Mechanism: "Weekday buyers drive past on the way to work; Saturday buyers come on foot. The rules moved the cars and not the feet, so what the bakery lost is the drivers and what it kept is the walkers."

Hand-off (Part 4 will name it): "Those walkers are the customers worth keeping."

Chain check, first noun phrase of each sentence: "The bakery" (claim) leads to "The month after the rules changed" (picks up "the new parking rules"), leads to "Weekday buyers" (picks up "weekday-morning sales"), leads to "The rules" (picks up "the rules changed"), leads to "Those walkers" (picks up "the walkers"). Linear shape, held throughout.

Test: underline the first noun phrase of every sentence after the first. Each one must point back to something already on the page. An underlined phrase that points nowhere is where the reader lost the thread.

Part 4. The last sentence and the break

Fact 4: the break is where the reader decides. The last sentence is the last thing they hold when they decide, and it is what they carry into the next paragraph. It has one job: hand off.

Three ways to end, and any one of them works:

  1. The consequence. What the idea means now that it is supported. One sentence, no new evidence.
  2. The bridge. Name the element the next paragraph's first sentence will pick up as its known part. This is how paragraphs chain and how the essay's shuffle test gets its teeth.
  3. Nothing. Stop on the strongest mechanism or evidence sentence. A paragraph does not owe the reader a closing line.

Four ways not to end:

  • A summary of the paragraph. The first sentence already did that. Restating it tells the reader you did not trust the middle.
  • A new claim. That is the next paragraph's first sentence, misplaced.
  • A rhetorical question. It hands the reader work instead of a result.
  • A hedge. "At least in most cases" as the final words makes the qualifier the thing they carry forward.

Readers weight the end of a paragraph the way they weight the end of a sentence. Put the strongest word last. Do not trail off.

When to break. Break when the support for the first sentence is complete, and not before. Not at a word count. Not because the block looks long. If it looks long, one of two things is true: there are two ideas (find the second first sentence hiding in the middle and split there), or the middle has padding (cut it). Inserting a break at a pleasing spot fixes neither and breaks Fact 4's promise.

The one-sentence paragraph is legitimate. It is emphasis, and emphasis works by contrast. Twice on one page and it is a tic; the reader stops trusting the white space.

Live example. Paragraph 2 ended: "Those walkers are the customers worth keeping." Paragraph 3 opens: "A second kind of customer is appearing, the one who walks from the new flats, and the bakery has not learned to serve them yet." The word "customer" is the hand-off. Read the pair together and it runs as two consecutive sentences.

The same paragraph with the endings not to use:

  • Summary: "So the bakery is losing its weekday customers to parking." Already said, sentence one.
  • Hedge: "Of course, this may differ by shop." The reader leaves holding "may differ."
  • Question: "But what does that mean for my shop?" The reader was about to find out; now they have to ask.

Test: read the last sentence of a paragraph and the first sentence of the next as a pair, out of context. If they read as two consecutive sentences, the hand-off is there. If there is a jolt, one of them is missing its known part.

Part 5. Build procedure and three tests

From a first sentence to a finished paragraph:

1. Claim       one sentence; deniable; the new thing at the end
2. Questions   write "how do you know?" and "why does that follow?" under it
3. Evidence    one specific thing that answers the first question
4. Mechanism   one sentence that answers the second
5. Shape       pick linear or hub; reorder so each sentence opens on a known
6. Hand-off    consequence, bridge, or stop on the strongest sentence
7. Strike      delete any sentence that answers neither question

Write the two questions down. They are the only two things the middle may answer, and seeing them on the page is what stops the second claim, the hedge, and the background from getting in.

Step 7 is delete-only. If the paragraph is still long after striking, there are two ideas in it. Go back to step 1 with the second one.

Three tests

  1. Summary test. Say the paragraph in one sentence. If it needs "and," split. If you cannot, there is no idea.
  2. Underline test. Underline the first noun phrase of every sentence after the first. Each must point back to something already on the page. One that points nowhere is the break in the chain.
  3. Pair test. Read the last sentence and the next paragraph's first sentence together. They must read as consecutive. A jolt means a missing hand-off or a missing known part.

Run them in that order. The first finds the idea, the second finds the chain, the third finds the joints between paragraphs.

When the rules bend

Narrative paragraphs open on a scene and may carry the idea implicitly; the underline test still applies, because a scene has its own chain of known to new. Dialogue and lists have their own shapes. Everything argumentative, technical, or explanatory: first sentence is the claim, middle answers two questions, end hands off.

Where this lands in the current work

Rosa's day-5 draft has the three first sentences from Part 2 as its outline. Each paragraph under the 800-word cap is one run of this procedure. The rent letter is the same procedure with the count as evidence and the parking change as mechanism.

Exercise

Take one paragraph from this week that you reread twice. Write "how do you know?" and "why does that follow?" under its first sentence. Mark each remaining sentence evidence, mechanism, or neither. Strike the neithers. If the first sentence is a topic rather than a claim, rewrite it before anything else. Run the three tests.
