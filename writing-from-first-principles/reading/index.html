Part 1. Two facts about the reader, and what comprehension is

Uses Facts 7 and 8 from The Reader: comprehension is a model, and the model lives in working memory.

The writing chapters (2 to 4) each rest on two facts about the reader. This one rests on two about the reader as a builder rather than a receiver.

Fact 7. Comprehension is not decoding. Decoding turns marks into words; comprehension builds a model of what the writer claims and how they support it, then checks that model against what you already hold. The two can come apart completely. Every word on a page can be decoded while the model never updates. That is the exact experience in my reading adaptation notes: "words don't register on first read." The decoding happened. The model did not.

Fact 8. The model is built in working memory, and working memory is small (the sentence chapter's Fact 5, about twenty words). So a reader cannot hold a page. They hold a scaffold, the claim and the shape of the argument, and file details into it as they arrive. A detail with no slot to land in falls out within a sentence. This is why the structure-first pass in the adaptation notes works: it builds the slots before the details arrive.

From these, what comprehension is:

Being able to state, in your own words, what the writer claims, how they support it, and where you would object.

Three parts, and all three are production, not feeling. The feeling of having understood is unreliable; fluent decoding produces it whether or not a model exists. The only test is whether you can say it back without the page.

Two consequences that run through the rest of this chapter:

  • Reading an argument is the essay chapter run backward. The writer built claim, then objections in reader order, then evidence and mechanism, then sentences. The reader recovers them in the same order: claim first, then the shape, then the support. Where the writer did the job, this is fast. Where the writer did not (claim buried at the end, topics instead of claims), the reader must build the outline the writer skipped, and knowing that is half the work.
  • The small words are operators on the model. "Not," "only," "except," "unless," "additional," "excluding" do not add detail; they flip or bound the claim. Skipping one leaves a model that is wrong in a way that feels right. The kill-word scan in the adaptation notes is not a test-taking trick. It is model protection, and it belongs in every reading where the claim matters. The verbal section of a timed assessment, 18 of 18 with that scan in place, is the evidence on file.

One consequence to use today. Rosa's day-5 reread found her diary went tight in observation mode and loose in forecast mode. Reading has the same two modes. When the text reports what happened, decoding and comprehension travel together. When the text forecasts or argues, they separate, and the model has to be built on purpose. Notice which mode the text is in before you decide how to read it.

Part 2. Before reading: the question and the map

Fact 8 says details need slots. Slots come from two things you can build before the first full sentence: a question and a map. Skipping this step is how a reader arrives at paragraph three with nothing to attach it to.

The question. Why are you reading this, in one sentence, with a verb. Not "to learn about X." "To decide whether to apply," "to find the one setting that changes the timeout," "to check whether the author's evidence supports the title." The question sets the mode, and the mode sets the method:

Purpose                          Mode           Method
Decide or act on it              Mastery        full read, model built, objections written
Know that it exists, roughly     Exposure       first sentences only, one-line summary, stop
Find one thing                   Locating       scan headings and kill-words for the target, read that paragraph only

These are three reading modes matched to purpose, put to work. Most comprehension failures are mode failures: reading a mastery text at exposure speed, or giving a locating task a full read and drowning the target in context. Name the mode before you start, and you have already fixed most of them.

The map. Before the full read, the five-second pass from the adaptation notes: first sentence of every paragraph, headings, anything in a table or code block. You are not reading yet. You are building slots. From the pass, write down, in one line each:

  1. What the writer probably claims. A guess. It will be wrong in a useful way.
  2. What the writer will have to prove for the claim to hold. Two or three items.
  3. Where you already disagree, or what you already know that bears on it.

Item 1 is the slot for the claim. Item 2 is the slots for the evidence. Item 3 is the check you will run at the end (Part 4). A guess written down before reading is the only way to notice afterward that the text changed your mind, or failed to.

If the first-sentence pass yields no claim, only topics, the writer did not put the claim first. Expect it at the end, or in the title, or nowhere. Mark that now. You will be building the outline the writer skipped, and the reading will cost more than its length suggests.

The operator scan belongs here too. Before the full read, run the kill-word scan over the passage that carries the claim: not, only, except, unless, additional, excluding, must, cannot, however. Mark each one. Every mark is a place where the model can flip. Doing it before the read means the eye arrives at those words already expecting them, instead of skating past.

Live example. The council's parking notice opens on a claim: from March, cars on the high street move to the side streets. Question: "Will the new rules cut my weekday morning trade?" Mode: mastery, since a decision hangs on it. Map from first sentences: claim (parking moves), proofs needed (which hours, which days, which streets), prior knowledge (weekday buyers drive; Saturday buyers walk). Kill-words in the claim paragraph: "except" and "only." With that in hand, the full read files every hour and street into a slot, and the one line that matters ("except on market days") lands as the answer to the question instead of one sentence among forty.

Test: before the full read, can you say in one line what you expect the writer to claim and what would change your mind? If not, you have not started reading yet, whatever your eyes have done.

Part 3. During reading: the writer's structure in reverse

The map is built. Now the full read, and the job during it is to fill the slots and to notice when the text refuses to fit them. The essay chapter gave the writer three things to place: the claim, the objections in reader order, the evidence with its mechanism. The reader recovers the same three, in the same order.

Find the claim. In a well-built text it is the first sentence of the first or second paragraph, and the map already caught it. Confirm it. If the map guessed wrong, cross out the guess and write the real one; the correction is comprehension happening. If the claim never appears in the opening, read the last paragraph now, before the middle. Writers who bury the claim bury it at the end. Getting it from there first turns the middle from a mystery into support.

Find the objections. Each paragraph's first sentence is the writer's answer to a "but" the reader might raise. Read it and name the but. "But he said no" sits behind "the reply says the letter was general." Naming the objection tells you what the paragraph is for, and a paragraph you know the purpose of is a paragraph whose details have slots. A first sentence that answers no objection you can name is either padding or a paragraph whose claim you have not yet found. Mark it and move on. Do not reread the whole paragraph; reread only the sentence that did not register, then continue (this is the adaptation notes' rule and it holds here).

Find the evidence and the mechanism. Inside each paragraph, the reader asks the paragraph chapter's two questions of the writer: how do you know, and why does that follow. Evidence is the specific thing: a number, an incident, a quote. Mechanism is the link. When both are present, the paragraph has done its work and you can go on. When evidence is present without mechanism, mark it: the writer is asking for trust. When neither is present, the first sentence was an assertion, and it goes into the objections you will write in Part 4.

The operators are where the adaptation matters most. The kill-words marked in Part 2 are on the page ahead of you; when the eye reaches one, stop for a beat and restate the sentence with the operator made explicit. "Not X" becomes "X is false." "Only X" becomes "X and nothing else." "Unless Y" becomes "if Y does not hold." The restatement takes two seconds and is the only guard against a model that is confidently inverted. My reading adaptation notes trace the numerical-section errors on the same assessment to exactly this: eyes skating past a header or a footnote that bounded the claim.

Pace. The notes' mechanics apply during this pass, and they are means to the model, not ends: finger or cursor under the line, chunks of two or three words, a beat at every period so the sentence consolidates before the next one overwrites it. Subvocalize where the text is dense and the mode is mastery; my own survey of the reading-methods literature found no evidence that suppressing the inner voice helps comprehension, and some that it costs word-level understanding. The leak is encoding, not speed.

What to write while reading. Little. One word in the margin per paragraph: the objection it answers, or a question mark if you could not name one, or an exclamation mark where the text contradicted your map. Anything longer than a word stops the read and empties working memory; Part 4 is where the writing happens.

Live example. The shopkeepers' letter to the council, read in mastery mode. Claim, first paragraph: the high street lost trade to the parking change, not to the new mall. Objections behind the next paragraphs: "but the mall opened the same month" (answer: the mall's own foot count rose 3 percent while the street's fell 20, a table), "but prices are the complaint" (answer: the average receipt did not move at the three shops that counted), "but this is just a mood" (answer: "timing flag: this letter was drafted three days after the rent notices went out," the writer naming the pattern on themselves). Operators caught: "only cause," "not the mall," "unless the bays return." Each restated. The model at the end of the read: one cause named, two ruled out, one ask made, and a warning not to decide from the notice. That model fits the question, and the evidence for each part is locatable.

Test: at the end of each paragraph, can you say which objection it answered and whether it gave evidence, mechanism, both, or neither? If you cannot, the paragraph passed through decoding without touching the model. Reread its first sentence only, and try again.

Part 4. After reading: reproduce, then object

Fact 7 made comprehension a model, and Part 1 said the only test of a model is production. So the reading is not finished when the eyes reach the end. It is finished when you have produced the model without the page and checked it against what you brought in.

Reproduce first. Close the text. Write, from memory, in your own words:

  1. The claim, one sentence.
  2. The support, two or three items: what the writer offered as evidence and the mechanism that linked it.
  3. The one line you would quote if you could quote only one.

Own words is the whole point. Copying the writer's sentence proves decoding; restating it proves a model. If you reach for the page in the first minute, the model is not there yet. Reread the paragraph whose slot is empty, not the whole text, and try again.

Compare to the map. Put the Part 2 guess next to the reproduction. Three outcomes:

  • The guess matched. The text confirmed what you expected. Ask whether you read it or recognized it; recognition feels like comprehension and skips the evidence. Check that you can name the support, not just the claim.
  • The guess was wrong and the text corrected it. This is the reading that paid. Write the correction as a sentence: "I expected X; the writer showed Y because Z."
  • The guess was wrong and you did not notice until now. The model was built from the map, not the text. Go back to the paragraph that should have corrected you and find the sentence you skated past. It is often the one with the operator.

Then object. The third part of the definition. Where would you push back, and on what grounds? Three kinds of objection, in order of value:

  1. Evidence. The writer claimed and did not show, or showed something that does not carry the claim. Name the paragraph.
  2. Mechanism. The evidence is real but the link to the claim is asserted, not argued. Say what else could explain the evidence.
  3. Scope. The claim is true for the case shown and the writer widened it. Name the boundary.

An objection you can state is comprehension at its strongest, because it means you hold the writer's model and your own at once and can see where they differ. A text you cannot object to is either flawless or not yet understood.

Then decide what to keep. One line. What changed in what you believe or will do, or "nothing, and here is why." This is the Second Brain resonance filter, run at the end instead of during: capture what changed a model, not what sounded good. A note that carries the claim, the one quote, and the objection is worth more than a highlighted page.

Live example. Rosa's day-5 reread of her own diary, in mastery mode. Reproduction: claim, the diary's forecast outruns its evidence (two regulars gone in one month, one landlord reply on file); support, the till counts and the reply text; the one quote, "Customers are not the shortage; counting is." Compared to the map: the guess was "the diary is loose everywhere," and the text corrected it: the writing is tight in observation mode and loose in forecast mode, which is a narrower and more useful claim. Objection, scope: the reread treats one month of till counts as evidence against "the whole street is dying," but one month is not yet a trend either way. Kept: the observation-versus-forecast split, which became the evidence rule in the paragraph chapter the same day.

Test: without the page, say the claim, two supports, and one objection out loud in under a minute. If any of the four is missing, name the missing slot and go back for that paragraph only.

Part 5. Build procedure, three tests, and the adaptation

The four parts as one procedure, for a text that matters:

1. Question    one sentence with a verb; pick the mode (mastery / exposure / locating)
2. Map         first sentences, headings, tables; write guess, proofs needed, prior view
3. Operators   kill-word scan over the claim-bearing passage; mark each
4. Read        confirm claim; name each paragraph's "but"; ask how-do-you-know and why-follows
5. Restate     at each marked operator, say the sentence with the operator explicit
6. Reproduce   close the text; claim, two supports, one quote, in your own words
7. Object      evidence, mechanism, or scope; one line
8. Keep        what changed, or "nothing, and why"

Steps 1 to 3 build slots. Steps 4 and 5 fill them. Steps 6 to 8 prove the model exists. Exposure mode runs steps 1, 2, and a one-line 6. Locating mode runs steps 1, 3, and reads one paragraph. Only mastery runs all eight.

Three tests

  1. Map test. Before the full read, one line on what you expect the writer to claim and what would change your mind. Cannot write it: you have not started.
  2. Paragraph test. After each paragraph, which objection did it answer, and did it give evidence, mechanism, both, or neither. Cannot say: reread its first sentence only.
  3. Production test. Without the page, claim, two supports, one objection, under a minute. A missing slot names the paragraph to return to.

Run them in order. The first catches reading without a purpose, the second catches decoding without modeling, the third catches the feeling of understanding standing in for understanding.

The adaptation: choosing the method from actual recall

My reading-methods survey named a gap: a protocol for choosing scan, intensive read, reread, glossary, or annotation from the reader's real recall and inference, not from a rule about the inner voice. The production test is that protocol. Run step 6 and read the result:

Production test result                          Method next time on this kind of text
Claim and supports present, objection present   Current method is right; do not slow down
Claim present, supports missing                 Intensive read: add the paragraph test after each block
Claim present, one support inverted             Operator problem: run the kill-word scan first, restate at each mark
Claim wrong or missing                          Map problem: the first-sentence pass was skipped or the writer buried the claim; read the close first
Words decoded, nothing reproducible             Encoding problem: pacing under the line, chunks, a beat at each period, subvocalize; slower is the fix
Unknown terms blocked the model                 Glossary before the read (precompute aids), not stopping mid-sentence

This is how my reading adaptation notes' mechanics stop being a fixed regimen and become responses to a measurement. The mechanics are the same ones that scored 18 of 18 in March. What changes is that each one is now attached to the failure it repairs, so the ones a given text does not need can be left off.

Two things the adaptation does not require. It does not require reading faster; every line of the notes says the leak is encoding. And it does not require suppressing the inner voice; my own check of the literature found no evidence that suppression helps and some that it costs word-level understanding. Subvocalize where the production test says the model is thin, and skip it where the test says the model is fine.

Where this lands

A landlord, a councillor, or an editor reads your letter this way: claim in the first sentences, a "but" behind each paragraph, evidence and mechanism inside, and an objection ready if the mechanism is missing. Writing to the essay, paragraph, and sentence chapters produces text that survives this reading. Reading this way finds, in other people's text, the places their writing did not.

The four chapters are one system. The reader builds slots, fills them, and proves the model by producing it. Where the two meet is the one line the reader can quote back.

Exercise

Take one text you would normally hand to a model to summarize: a letter from the council, an article, a chapter of a book. Run the eight steps on paper. Write the reproduction (step 6) before you open any model. If you then ask a model for a summary, put its claim next to yours and let the text decide which one it supports. Record the result in the day's journal, one line.
