2. Essay Structure

Map

Part 1. Two facts about the reader

Uses Facts 1 and 2 from The Reader: the reader can stop, and the reader does not have your head.

An essay moves one reader from not believing something to believing it. Everything about structure follows from two facts about that reader.

Fact 1. The reader can stop at any sentence. Nobody is obliged to reach paragraph two. So the essay cannot save its point for the end. Each paragraph must buy the next one.

Fact 2. The reader does not have your head. They did not do your research, in your order, with your context. So the order of the essay is the order in which the reader needs things, not the order in which you found them.

From these two facts, one rule:

Say the claim first. Then give the reader, in the order they would ask for it, what they need to believe it.

Every structural device in the rest of this chapter is a consequence of that rule. Topic sentences, the objection chain, where evidence goes, why the close is not a summary. None of it is convention. It is what the two facts force.

One consequence to use today. The 0904 journal entry moved from "leads have not panned out" to "no one wants to hire me" in one line. A reader who stops there leaves with a verdict and no reason. Under the rule, the claim comes first and the reader's first objection comes second: "how many leads, and what did they say?" That objection is the next paragraph. That is the whole method in miniature.

Part 2. The claim

Start at the top of the hierarchy: do you have something to say? Everything below this level is polish on nothing until the answer is yes.

A claim is one sentence, with a verb, that a reasonable reader could deny. A topic is not a claim. "AI and programming jobs" is a topic. "Employers will hire fewer programmers because agents do the typing" is a claim. Test: put "I disagree" in front of the sentence. If that makes no sense, you have a topic.

Write the claim before anything else. One sentence. If you cannot, you have notes, not an essay. Notes are fine. Do not structure them yet.

The claim alone is not enough. Fact 1 says the reader can leave. They stay only if not knowing costs them something. So the second sentence you write is the cost: what the reader keeps doing, paying, or believing wrongly without the claim. Form: "If you believe X, you will do Y and lose Z." The cost is the reader's problem, not your knowledge. Knowledge you hold that solves no problem of theirs is trivia to them, however hard you worked for it.

Claim plus cost is the thesis. Not "in this essay I will explore." Not a question. A sentence someone could lose money or time by ignoring.

Three tests on the claim before you write a body:

  1. Deniable. A reader could say no.
  2. Specific. It names a mechanism or a who. "Things are changing" fails. "Employers hire fewer programmers because one senior plus agents ships what five did" passes.
  3. Yours. You can defend it from evidence you hold. If the evidence is someone else's, cite them and write about what you add.

One claim per essay. If the claim needs "and," you have two essays. Split.

The trap: the claim usually arrives last, as the conclusion of your notes. That is where you found it. It is not where the reader needs it. Move it to the top.

Live example. Claim: "The founder-interview miss was a specificity gap, not an experience gap." Deniable: the author denied it that morning. Specific: names the miss and the mechanism. Yours: the rejection text and 43 prep files are on file. Cost: "If you believe it was experience, you spend the window on courses and lose the window." That pair is the whole thesis.

Part 3. The body as an objection chain

The thesis is on the table. The reader has not accepted it. Fact 2: they do not have your head, so they are now saying "but." The body exists to answer those buts, in the order they arise.

Procedure:

  1. Write the thesis at the top of a blank page.
  2. Below it, list every objection a skeptical reader would raise. Write them as the reader would say them, starting with "but."
  3. Order them by when they arise. The first is the one that would make the reader stop.
  4. Each objection becomes one paragraph. No paragraph without an objection behind it.

Paragraph anatomy, in order:

  • First sentence: your answer to the objection. This is the topic sentence. A reader who reads only first sentences should get the whole argument.
  • Evidence: one specific thing. A number, an incident, a quote, a file. Not an adjective.
  • Mechanism: why that evidence supports the answer. This is the sentence writers skip. Evidence without mechanism is a list.
  • Optional last sentence: what this buys the next paragraph.

Test for one idea per paragraph: summarize it in one sentence. If you cannot, split. If you can, that sentence should already be the first one.

Test for order: shuffle the paragraphs. If nobody would notice, there is no chain. Each paragraph must depend on the one before it.

Evidence rules. The 0904 prose read found the pattern: writing about what was observed (eye twitch, sleep, a rejection) was tight; writing about the future slid into passive voice, abstractions, and questions. Stay in observation mode as long as you can. When you must forecast, name the mechanism, not the trend.

The strongest objection gets the most honest paragraph. State it better than the objector would. Then answer it, or concede it. Conceding narrows the claim. A narrower true claim beats a wider one the reader does not believe.

Length: the body is as long as the objections and no longer. Then one delete-only pass.

Live example. Thesis: the founder-interview miss was a specificity gap, not an experience gap. Objections in reader order, each one paragraph:

  1. "But they rejected you. That is what inexperience looks like." Answer: the rejection text says high-level and missed finer details. The demo got you past the recruiter to the CTO. Experience bought the seat; the interview lost it.
  2. "But the other candidates had more experience." Answer: they had numbers and incidents from something they had run. Forty-three prep files produced abstraction. That is specificity from operating, not years.
  3. "Then how does anyone get specific?" Answer: one operated system with a fail-class ledger. Mechanism: incidents with numbers are the only thing that survives a follow-up question.
  4. "Is that not experience by another name?" Concede: it is operating experience, measured in incidents, not in years. The claim narrows to "the missing thing is an operated system, not time served," and is stronger for it.

The close comes next.

Part 4. Opening and close: derived, not decorated

Both ends of the essay are forced by the two facts. Neither is a place for ceremony.

Opening

Fact 1 says the claim comes first. So the opening is the thesis from Part 2, cost and claim, in one or two paragraphs. Two orders:

  • Cost first, then claim, when the reader does not yet feel the problem. You make them feel it, then name the fix.
  • Claim first, then cost, when the reader already feels the problem. They came for the answer.

Not in the opening: history of the topic, definitions the reader does not need yet, a survey of what others think, "in this essay I will," and any sentence that points forward to a payoff instead of paying. The 0904 prose read caught two of these in one journal entry: "As expected," and three rhetorical questions in a row. Both are the writer warming up in front of the reader.

Test: delete the first paragraph. If the essay still works, delete it for real. Repeat until it does not.

The title is the claim compressed. If the title is a topic ("On experience"), the essay probably is too.

Close

The close is not a summary. A reader who reached the close already believes the claim, or has left. Summarizing spends attention they have already paid and signals you do not trust the body.

The close is the consequence: what changes now that the claim is true. What the reader does on Monday. If you conceded ground in the body, state the narrowed claim once, cleanly, as the thing that survived.

Not in the close: a restated payoff, a rhetorical question, "only time will tell," and the fake-profound antithesis ("it is not about X, it is about Y") that AI prose leans on.

Test: does the close contain a verb the reader can perform? If not, it is a summary.

Live example

Opening, cost first: "A founder rejected me last week for answers that were 'very high-level.' I spent the next three days believing I lacked experience. I was wrong, and the mistake would have cost me the hiring window. The miss was a specificity gap: I had a design and seven red teams, and the other candidates had numbers from something they had run."

Close, consequence: "Ship one operated system with a fail-class ledger before the next founder round. Bring one number per line of the job description. If you already run a fleet, mine it for incidents this week. That answer is available now, and no course sells it."

Part 5. Build procedure and three tests

The four parts above, run in order, from a blank page:

1. Claim        one sentence, a verb, deniable, specific, yours
2. Cost         "If you believe X, you will do Y and lose Z"
3. Objections   list every "but," in the order the reader raises them
4. Paragraphs   one per objection: answer, one piece of evidence, mechanism
5. Close        the consequence; a verb the reader can perform
6. Opening      cost and claim; write it last, choose the order
7. Title        the claim, compressed
8. Trim         delete-only pass; nothing added

Write the opening last. You do not know what the reader needs first until the body exists.

The trim pass cuts four things and adds nothing: throat-clearing before the claim, sentences that point forward to a payoff, a summary after an example that already landed, and any restated payoff. This is the Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100) and Zinsser trim pass from my house style.

Three tests

  1. First-sentence test. Read only the first sentence of each paragraph. The argument must be complete. If a gap appears, a paragraph is missing or a topic sentence is buried.
  2. Shuffle test. Reorder the paragraphs. Someone must notice. If nobody would, there is no chain, only a list.
  3. Deletion test. Delete any one paragraph. The chain must break. If it does not, that paragraph goes.

Run them in that order. The first finds missing steps, the second finds a missing chain, the third finds padding.

When the rules bend

A personal essay may open with a scene instead of a stated claim. The scene is the cost, felt rather than named. The claim still exists; the writer knows it, and the reader gets it through what happens. The scaffolding-removal method for personal essays is this chapter run in reverse: each draft strips the explaining so the scenes carry the weight.

That is the only exception. A technical guide, an argument, a reference page, an agency writing sample: claim first.

Where this lands in the current work

The agency writing sample is a technical guide. Same shape: the claim is what to do, the cost is what breaks otherwise, the objections are the reader's questions in order, the close is the command they run. The interview answer bank is the same shape at paragraph size: answer, number, mechanism.

The 0904 journal entry is the counterexample on file. Rebuilt under this procedure it becomes one claim ("employment's door is closing, so I will sell writing"), one cost, and the objections the 0904 review already listed: three full-time applications live, one rejection on file, 63 pieces published and no editor test yet. Whether the claim survives its own objections is the essay.

Exercise

Take the most recent journal entry that argues something. Write its claim in one sentence and its cost in one more. List the objections in the order a skeptical reader would raise them. Rebuild the entry as one paragraph per objection, answer first. Run the three tests on the result and keep the one that failed as the note for next time.
