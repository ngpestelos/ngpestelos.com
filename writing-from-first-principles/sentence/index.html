Part 1. Two facts about the reader, and what a sentence is

Uses Facts 5 and 6 from The Reader: about twenty words of working memory, and the reader hunts for who did what.

Fact 5. The reader holds a sentence in working memory until it resolves. Working memory is small: about twenty words of ordinary prose before the beginning starts to fade. A sentence longer than that is not wrong, but it asks the reader to carry the opening while waiting for the verb. Most readers drop it.

Fact 6. The reader parses a sentence by looking for two things: who, and did what. The moment they find the actor and the action, the sentence has a shape and everything else attaches to it. Until they find them, every word is held in suspense. A sentence that delays its actor and verb, or hides them in nouns, makes the reader do the parsing the writer skipped.

From these, what a sentence is:

One complete thought, with a named actor and a real verb, resolved before the reader's memory runs out.

"Complete thought" is Level 2 of the hierarchy and it means the sentence can be judged true or false on its own. "Named actor and real verb" is Fact 6. "Before memory runs out" is Fact 5, and it is why the house rule says about twenty words.

Three consequences that will run through the rest of this chapter:

  • The verb is the sentence. Find the verb first; a sentence with a weak verb has no spine, whatever its nouns are doing.
  • Resolution is the goal, not length. A twelve-word sentence that never names who acted is harder than a twenty-five-word sentence that does in the first six.
  • One thought per sentence for the same reason as one idea per paragraph: two thoughts joined by "and" ask the reader to hold both, and Fact 3 from the paragraph chapter says they hold one.

One consequence to use right away. From Rosa's day-4 diary: "Most of the bread on this street will be bought by people in cars but decided by where they can park." Twenty-one words, so the length is fine. But the reader waits until word nine for a verb, and the verb is "be bought," which names no one doing anything. The actors are in the last words. Turned around: "Drivers buy most of the bread on this street; parking decides where they stop." Actor, verb, object, twice. The reader parses it in one pass.

Part 2. The spine: who does what

Fact 6 says the reader hunts for the actor and the action. Give them both, early, and in that order. That pair is the spine. Everything else in the sentence hangs from it.

Two rules make the spine:

  1. The grammatical subject names the actor. The thing that did something is the subject of the sentence, not an abstraction standing in for it. "The decision was made to leave" has a subject ("the decision") that did nothing. "I decided to leave" has one that did.
  2. The verb names the action. Not "is," "has," "was made," "took place," "occurred." Those verbs report that something exists. The action is usually hiding nearby, as a noun.

The hiding place is the nominalization: a verb turned into a noun. "Decision" hides "decide." "Failure" hides "fail." "Implementation" hides "implement." "Analysis" hides "analyze." When the action is a noun, the sentence needs a filler verb to carry it, the actor drops out or moves into a "by" phrase, and the reader gets abstraction where they wanted an event.

The repair is mechanical. Find the noun that used to be a verb. Turn it back. Ask who did it. Make that the subject.

  • "The failure of the letter was due to a lack of numbers." Nominalization: failure. Verb: fail. Who failed? The letter. "The letter failed because it had no numbers."
  • "There was a realization on my part that the lease was at risk." Nominalization: realization. Verb: realize. Who? I. "I realized the lease was at risk."
  • "Drafting produced adjectives." Already a spine. Leave it.

The passive voice is the same problem in another form: the actor is missing or demoted to the end. "The letter was judged general" hides who judged. "The landlord judged the letter general" does not. Passive is correct in exactly two cases: the actor is unknown or does not matter ("the file was deleted overnight"), or the paragraph's chain needs the object in the known-first position (Part 3). Everywhere else it is a spine with the head cut off.

Distance. Once the actor appears, the verb should follow within a few words. A subject separated from its verb by a long clause makes the reader hold the subject in suspense, which is Fact 5's cost paid twice. "The baker who had spent three weeks drafting the letter while the shop lost its weekday customers to the new parking rules sent it." Nineteen words between "baker" and "sent." Move the clause: "The baker sent the letter after three weeks of drafting; meanwhile the shop had lost its weekday customers to the new parking rules."

Abstract subjects are a spine with a ghost in the actor's place. "The whole street changing" cannot do anything. Drivers, the council, or you can. When a sentence's subject is an abstraction, ask who is inside it, and put them there.

Live example, the third first sentence from the paragraph chapter: "A second kind of customer is appearing, the one who walks from the new flats, and the bakery has not learned to serve them yet." Two spines joined by "and," and the first one is weak: "customer is appearing" has a filler verb and an abstraction as actor. Split and repair: "The new flats are sending a second kind of customer: the one who walks. The bakery has not learned to serve them yet." Actor, verb, object; then actor, verb, object.

Test: circle the subject and the verb of the sentence. Is the subject something that can act? Is the verb something it can do? If either answer is no, look for the nominalization and turn it back.

Part 3. Order: known first, new last

The same rule has appeared at every level. Inside the sentence it is the rule for word order.

Two positions in a sentence carry weight. The opening, where the reader looks for the actor and for the link to what came before. The end, where the reader's attention lands and stays; the last few words are what they remember and what they carry into the next sentence. Everything between is transit.

So:

  • Put the known at the start. The subject should usually be something the reader already has from the previous sentence. That is why the passive is sometimes right (Part 2): when the object is the known thing, leading with it keeps the chain, even at the cost of the actor.
  • Put the new at the end. The word or phrase you want the reader to hold goes last. Not the qualifier, not the citation, not the "however."
  • Keep the middle short. Modifiers, asides, and parentheticals between subject and verb, or between verb and object, are transit the reader pays for.

Three moves that follow:

  1. Reorder for the end. "The landlord said no because the letter had no numbers" and "Because the letter had no numbers, the landlord said no" carry the same facts. The first is about the missing numbers; the second is about the no. Choose by what the next sentence needs.
  2. Move the connective off the end. "The letter got a reply in two days, however" leaves the reader holding "however." "The letter, however, got a reply in two days" leaves them holding two days. Better still, drop the connective if the previous sentence already set up the contrast.
  3. Trail nothing. "Drivers buy most of the bread on this street, at least on weekdays, for now." Two qualifiers after the point. The reader keeps "for now." If the qualifiers matter, they go in front: "On weekdays, drivers buy most of the bread on this street."

Do not confuse this with burying the verb. Known-first means the subject is familiar, not that it is long. A short familiar subject, a verb within a few words, then the new material building to the end. The spine from Part 2 sits near the front; the weight sits at the back.

Lists inside a sentence follow the same rule at small scale. Order items from shortest to longest, or from known to new, so the sentence gathers rather than dribbles. "Receipts, a door count, and a month ledger with totals per week" reads better than the reverse.

Live example, from the paragraph chapter's middle: "The rules moved the cars and not the feet, so what the bakery lost is the drivers and what it kept is the walkers." Known first: "the rules" picks up "the rules changed" from the sentence before. New last: "the walkers" is the phrase the next sentence needs ("Those walkers"). The middle is the mechanism, in transit. Nothing trails after the point.

Test: read the last three words of each sentence in a paragraph, and nothing else. They should be the words that matter. If they are "however," "at least," "in most cases," or a citation, the sentence ends before its point or after it.

Part 4. Cutting: what a sentence does not need

Fact 5 gives the budget: about twenty words before the opening fades. Every word that is not the spine, the known link, or the new point spends that budget on nothing. Cutting returns memory to the reader so the point arrives before they drop the beginning.

This is Zinsser's clutter and Simplified Technical English's one-meaning rule, run at word scale. Seven classes, each with the test that finds it:

  1. Throat-clearing openers. "It is worth noting that," "As expected," "The fact is," "In my view." Test: delete the words before the subject. The sentence is the same, and shorter.
  2. Hedges and intensifiers. "Somewhat," "quite," "very," "really," "arguably," "in a sense," "to some extent." Test: delete the word. If the sentence now claims more than you believe, you did not want a hedge; you wanted a narrower claim. Write that instead. "Arguably the best" means you have not decided. Decide.
  3. Doublets. "Each and every," "first and foremost," "clear and obvious," "skills and experience." Test: one of the pair covers it. Keep that one.
  4. Empty subjects. "There is," "there are," "it is ... that." Test: find the real actor later in the sentence and start there. "There are three reasons the letter failed" becomes "The letter failed for three reasons."
  5. Nominalizations and their filler verbs (Part 2). "Made a decision," "conducted an analysis," "reached a conclusion," "gave consideration to." Test: one verb does the job. Decided. Analyzed. Concluded. Considered.
  6. Redundant modifiers. "Future plans," "past history," "completely finished," "personal opinion," "end result." Test: the noun already contains the adjective.
  7. Metaphor stacks and cliches. "The street is dying," "the writing is on the wall," "somehow keep the doors open." Test: say what happened in plain words. Rosa's day-5 reread found all three of those in one diary entry; the plain versions were "two regulars stopped coming," "the landlord has not said yes," and "I do not know the next step."

Two cuts that are not cuts:

  • Length is not the target. A twenty-eight word sentence with a clear spine and one thought can stay. Split a sentence only when it holds two thoughts (find the "and" or the semicolon that joins two spines) or when the subject and verb are far apart (Part 2).
  • Plain words are not short words. Simplified Technical English asks for one clear meaning per word, not for monosyllables. "Nominalization" is the right word above because it has one meaning; "thing" would be shorter and worse.

Passive voice is on this list only when it hides the actor. When it keeps the known-first order (Part 3), it stays.

The pass is delete-only. You are not rewriting; you are removing. Rewriting comes back to Parts 2 and 3 if the sentence still fails after the cut. Do the cut first, because most sentences do not need the rewrite once the clutter is gone.

Live example. From the day-4 diary: "I'm almost out of ideas, but one thing remains: the numbers. Now I finally have the time to count enough to be able to make my case." Cuts: "almost" (hedge; you are out or you are not), "finally" (intensifier), "enough to be able to" (four words for "to"). Result: "I am out of ideas but one thing remains: the numbers. Now I have the time to count and make my case." From 27 words to 22, same claim, and the weak spot shows: "count and make my case" is now visibly the gate the day-5 reread flagged, because the padding that softened it is gone. That is what cutting is for: it exposes.

Test: for each word, ask what is lost if it goes. If the answer is nothing, it goes. If the answer is "the sentence sounds less careful," it goes too; careful is a claim you narrow, not a word you add.

Part 5. Build procedure and three tests

For a sentence that is not working, in order:

1. Thought    say the one thing the sentence claims; if "and" joins two, split
2. Actor      who did it; make that the subject, not an abstraction
3. Verb       what they did; one real verb, unhidden from its noun
4. Distance   verb within a few words of the subject
5. Known      open on what the reader already holds
6. New        close on the phrase they must carry forward
7. Cut        delete-only pass, seven classes from Part 4

Steps 1 to 3 build the spine. Steps 5 and 6 place it. Step 7 clears what is left. Most sentences that fail need only step 7. Sentences that still fail after step 7 need steps 2 and 3, and it is the noun-that-was-a-verb almost every time.

You do not run this on every sentence you write. You run it on the sentence you reread twice. Fluency comes from having run it enough times that the spine arrives first without the procedure.

Three tests

  1. Spine test. Circle subject and verb. Can the subject act? Is the verb an action? Two yeses or rebuild.
  2. Last-words test. Read the final three words. Are they the point? If they are a qualifier, a connective, or a citation, reorder.
  3. Loss test. For each remaining word, what is lost if it goes? Nothing means it goes.

Run them in order. The first finds a missing spine, the second finds misplaced weight, the third finds clutter.

When the rules bend

Rhythm. A run of same-length spine-first sentences reads like a list of instructions. Vary length on purpose: a long sentence that carries a mechanism, then a short one that lands it. The short one obeys every rule above. The long one obeys them too, and may run past twenty words if the spine is early and the thought is one.

Passive for the chain (Part 3), fragments for emphasis in personal prose, a sentence that opens on the new thing to jolt the reader once. Each is a rule broken on purpose, once, with the default restored in the next sentence. Broken twice on a page it is a habit, not a choice.

Where this lands

The three chapters nest. The essay says the claim first and answers objections in reader order. Each objection is a paragraph: claim in the first sentence, two questions answered in the middle, hand-off at the end. Each sentence in that paragraph is a spine placed known-first and new-last, then cut.

Rosa's day-5 draft is where the three meet. Write under the cap first. Then run the essay tests on the whole, the paragraph tests on each block, and the sentence tests only on the sentences you reread.

Exercise

Take five sentences from your own writing that a reader would have to read twice. For each, circle the subject and the verb, find the noun that used to be a verb, rebuild the spine, then run the seven-class cut. Write the before and after word counts and, in a few words, what the cut exposed.
